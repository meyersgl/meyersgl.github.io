## Question
Should Chapter 4 restore the SNAP time-limit exemption for people experiencing homelessness, and how much of the 2025 reconciliation law's SNAP changes should the platform reverse?

## Decision
Option A, broadened: restore all exemptions and reverse every SNAP change in Pub. L. 119-21 (Sections 10101 to 10108).
- **Exemptions (Section 4.5.1):**
  - people experiencing homelessness (McKinney-Vento definition, with three months' continuation after housing);
  - veterans;
  - young adults aged 24 or younger who aged out of foster care;
  - any parent or household member responsible for a child under 18.
  - The 2025 additions (American Indians, Alaska, Hawaii) are kept.
- **Verification (Section 4.5.2):** homeless-services records or a provider statement, or the person's own statement if neither exists.
- **Funding (Section 4.5.4):** the federal government pays the full cost of benefits and again pays 50 percent of administrative costs.
- **Benefits (Section 4.5.5):**
  - Thrifty Food Plan re-evaluations without the cost-neutrality limit;
  - energy-assistance link to the utility allowance for all households;
  - internet costs count again;
  - SNAP-Ed restored.
- **Eligibility (Section 4.5.6):** refugees, asylees, trafficking survivors, and other pre-2025 qualified immigrant groups are eligible again.
- **Phase-in:** all part of the SNAP changes in General Fund tier 1, step 1 (Section 27.9).

## Supersedes
The caregiver rule in decision 0023 (children under 6; sole caregivers of children under 14), now broadened to any child under 18.

## Why
The 2023 exemptions were a bipartisan compromise, and the homelessness exemption aligns SNAP with Chapter 8's Housing First approach. The other 2025 changes cut benefits and shifted costs to states without improving work outcomes.
