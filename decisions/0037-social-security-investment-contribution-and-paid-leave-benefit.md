## Question
The trust-fund tests (Appendix E.4) found that eliminating the payroll-tax cap closes about 58 percent of Social Security's 75-year deficit of 4.42 percent of taxable payroll, leaving about 1.9 percent. The platform rules out benefit cuts, a higher retirement age, and rate increases below the taxable maximum, and the 60 percent wage ceiling leaves no room above it. Separately, Section 3.3 had no replacement rate for paid leave.

## Decision
- **S1, C.** The 10 percent surtax on investment income above $1 million (decision 0034) becomes a 12.4 percent Social Security contribution on the same base, paid to the Social Security trust funds with no added benefits (Sections 2.7.5 and 26.2.4).
- **P1, B.** Paid leave replaces 90 percent of wages up to half the national average weekly wage and 50 percent above it, up to about $1,500 a week, indexed (Section 3.3).

## Why
- Investment income of millionaires now contributes to Social Security on the same terms as wages, and the top rate on gains and dividends (55.8 percent) matches the top rate on wages before the health contribution.
- A progressive replacement rate lets lower-paid workers take leave, at a moderate rate of about 0.6 to 1.0 percent of taxable payroll.

## Trade-offs
- The contribution closes only about 0.2 to 0.6 percent of taxable payroll, leaving about 1.3 to 1.7 percent. The platform still does not claim 75-year solvency.
- The General Fund loses about $250 to $700 billion over ten years. In the conservative case, eviction prevention and preschool now wait.
- At 55.8 percent, the top rate on gains is further above the revenue-maximizing estimate of about 42 percent.

## Options considered
- S1: leave the gap open (A); an automatic rate backstop (B); this contribution (C, chosen); General Fund money (D).
- P1: two-thirds of pay up to $1,000 a week (A); progressive (B, chosen); full pay up to a cap (C).

## Supersedes
Decision 0034, item R4: the surtax's revenue now goes to Social Security, not the General Fund.
