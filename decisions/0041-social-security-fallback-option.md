## Question
The platform's revenue reforms leave about 1.3 to 1.7 percent of taxable payroll unfunded, and the white paper's Open Policy Question listed only revenue options for closing it. In review, Andrew Biggs (American Enterprise Institute) argued that most of the growth from benefit cuts comes from high earners' added saving, so restraining benefits at the top while keeping a strong minimum benefit could keep much of that growth without cutting benefits for the poor. He also pointed to his 2021 flat-benefit plan, scored by the Penn Wharton Budget Model, and to the model of a targeted anti-poverty benefit plus universal retirement accounts.

## Decision
- **Option B (refined).** Name one fallback in the white paper's Open Policy Question: slower benefit growth for the highest lifetime earners (for example, gradually lowering the 15 percent factor in the benefit formula), paired with a strong minimum benefit. It is unscored and not adopted in the platform.
- **Rule out a flat or mostly flat benefit.** PWBM found that one such design cut benefits about 46 percent for average earners, reduced hours worked, and raised debt for decades.
- **Acknowledge the linkage cost.** The white paper and Section 26.6 now say that earnings above the current maximum earn no added benefits, so the added contribution works as a pure tax on work.
- **Open questions only:** the minimum benefit left for scoring in decision 0009, and universal retirement accounts as a supplement to Social Security, not a replacement.

## Why
The platform had no non-revenue answer for the remaining gap. This option confines any benefit restraint to the households best able to replace it with their own saving and strengthens protection against poverty, which is the program's first purpose (decision 0040). Naming it without adopting it keeps the platform's principle intact until it is scored.

## Trade-off
Listing any benefit restraint departs from the white paper's statement that solvency should be achieved without reducing earned benefits; some supporters will see it as opening the door to cuts. The growth benefit of top-only restraint is a hypothesis: PWBM has not modeled such a package, and the research on who offsets private saving against Social Security is mixed.
