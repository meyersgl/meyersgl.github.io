The central proposition

A strong public foundation. A dynamic market economy.

The platform rejects a false choice between largely unrestricted markets and direct centralized state management. It proposes a public foundation for essential security and opportunity while relying on competitive markets, private initiative, and innovation to generate growth.

Its stated goals are universal opportunity, competitive economic growth, and democratic accountability. The framework is intended as a basis for evaluation and debate—not as a claim that every proposal has already been independently validated.

Governing philosophy

Three commitments guide the framework

The platform seeks to combine public capacity with individual initiative and market competition. These commitments are meant to operate together, not as isolated policy slogans.

Universal opportunityEstablish a dependable public foundation that supports security, access, and participation.
Competitive growthUse open markets, private enterprise, and innovation as engines of productivity and prosperity.
Democratic accountabilityMake public authority transparent, reviewable, fiscally responsible, and answerable to citizens.

Policy architecture

Selected policy frameworks

These summaries highlight selected mechanisms described in the platform. They are summaries, not substitutes for the full text, and they reflect the current edition (v1.2).

Healthcare infrastructure

  • Universal coverage: A publicly financed baseline plan covering 17 categories of care with no copays or deductibles, optional supplemental private insurance, and independent, privately operated providers. Phased in, starting with a Medicare buy-in.
  • Innovation: Variable market exclusivity of 7 to 15 years for new therapies, based on R&D investment, therapeutic benefit, and public-health impact, followed by a public patent option (public manufacturing rights and generic competition).
  • Rural access: Reimbursement adjusted by a geographic volatility index, plus stabilization payments intended to prevent closures of rural hospitals, critical access hospitals, and community clinics.

Social Security and long-term finance

  • Payroll-tax cap eliminated: All covered wages contribute. Earnings above the taxable maximum do not raise benefits, and no rate increase applies to earnings below it.
  • Pass-through income: Business income from partnerships, S-corporations, and sole proprietorships is covered on the same basis as wages, and structures designed mainly to avoid contributions face corrective taxation.
  • Investment income: Investment income above $1 million contributes 12.4 percent, the combined rate on wages, with no added benefits.
  • Endowment: A supplemental endowment funded only from payroll-reform revenue that exceeds scheduled benefits and required reserves, invested in passive, nonvoting index funds. Wealth-tax revenue is not used.
  • Honest limit: The full retirement age stays at 67, with no new means test. The reforms close an estimated 60 to 70 percent of the 75-year shortfall in the 2026 Trustees Report, not all of it, and solvency is not claimed until Social Security's actuaries score them.

Frontier AI governance

  • Containment and reporting: Dangerous capabilities are tested only in isolated environments, with independent evaluation before deployment, incident reporting within 15 days (24 hours for imminent harm), emergency suspension for hosted systems, and whistleblower protection.
  • Capability-based oversight: A training-compute threshold triggers evaluation; demonstrated capability on the Algorithmic Capability Index (such as autonomous cyber activity and self-replication attempts) sets the level of oversight. Thresholds are reviewed at least annually.
  • Auditing: Regulators may run randomized independent tests of deployed systems.
  • Accountability: Liability follows a reasonableness standard tied to the developer's published, independently reviewed safety boundaries. Oversight is housed at Commerce/NIST, with federal rules as a floor and one national incident-reporting standard.
  • White paper: A dedicated policy paper expands the Algorithmic Capability Index (ACI), containment standards, independent evaluation, incident reporting, and developer accountability framework.

Climate, energy, and infrastructure

  • Pollution pricing: A predictable, gradually increasing carbon fee collected where fossil fuels enter the economy, plus a measured methane fee on major sources, with 80 percent of fee revenue, plus all border-adjustment receipts, returned to households as equal per-person dividends.
  • Trade: A border carbon adjustment limited to steel, aluminum, and cement, intended to address emissions leakage.
  • Coordinated review: Federal coordination of review for nationally significant energy infrastructure, such as interstate transmission, with funded permitting staff and clear timelines. Environmental and safety reviews are not weakened.
  • Energy mix: A clean-energy mix of advanced nuclear, wind, solar, and hydroelectric power, tailored by region and supported by storage and transmission.

Democratic and constitutional accountability

  • Federal service age limits: A proposed constitutional age limit of 68 for federal elected officials and federal judges.
  • Campaign finance: A proposed constitutional framework allowing reasonable limits on independent political expenditures while preserving speech, press, and association rights.
  • Emergency powers: Emergency declarations expire automatically unless renewed by joint resolution of Congress, with limits on powers unrelated to the underlying emergency.

Fiscal discipline

Funding before expansion

The executive summary establishes a governing rule: major programs should not take effect without an identified funding source and an independent cost estimate. Implementation should be phased, and recurring obligations should be paired with durable revenue.

The master financing framework assigns every revenue stream to exactly one use, so no dollar is counted twice. Recurring programs draw only on stable revenue; the wealth tax first fills a recession-stabilization reserve and then sends a smoothed share to later-tier programs only, and carbon revenue goes 80 percent to household dividends, 10 percent to a time-limited transition fund, and 10 percent to one-time costs. See the financing chapter.

What the fiscal framework calls for

  • Independent, nonpartisan cost estimates before implementation.
  • Published revenue assessments and stated assumptions.
  • Phased implementation rather than unfunded immediate expansion.
  • Borrowing for productive long-term assets, with durable funding for ongoing obligations.
  • Clear rules for reserve capitalization, use, and replenishment.

Important distinction: A funding framework is a governing commitment, not proof that the proposals are currently revenue-neutral or fiscally sustainable. Edition v1.3 compares every program with published cost and revenue estimates in Appendix E: under favorable assumptions the platform's revenue funds every phase-in tier, and under conservative assumptions only the first tier and part of the second, with the rest waiting until revenue grows. These are comparable estimates, not independent scores, which remain necessary work.

From proposal to evaluation

A practical path toward policy readiness

The platform is broad. A credible path forward should sequence the work, expose assumptions, and make the unresolved questions visible.

  1. Define the policy mechanismsClarify eligibility, benefits, administration, enforcement, transition rules, and the agencies responsible for delivery.
  2. Model fiscal and economic effectsEstimate costs, revenues, distributional effects, growth impacts, and debt implications across conservative, central, and high-impact scenarios.
  3. Test legal and constitutional designObtain specialist analysis of federal authority, constitutional amendments, rights, institutional independence, and foreseeable litigation.
  4. Sequence implementationSeparate foundational near-term reforms from major structural changes and longer-term initiatives; identify dependencies and milestones.
  5. Publish review and revisionsDocument expert feedback, disagreements, evidence, changes made, and questions that remain unresolved.

Independent scrutiny

Peer review is invited

This project is being presented for critical evaluation. Agreement is not a prerequisite for useful feedback; identifying weak assumptions, unintended effects, and implementation barriers is the purpose of review.

Areas where expert review would be especially valuable

  • Public finance, tax design, and long-term fiscal sustainability.
  • Healthcare economics, insurance design, and provider payment.
  • Social Security actuarial analysis and reserve governance.
  • Constitutional law and administrative law.
  • Competition policy, energy regulation, and AI governance.
  • Public administration, transition costs, and implementation capacity.

Questions for reviewers

  • Are the proposed mechanisms clear and internally consistent?
  • What costs, incentives, or distributional effects are missing?
  • Which proposals require redesign or stronger evidence?
  • What legal or administrative obstacles are most significant?
  • What should be prioritized, delayed, or removed?
