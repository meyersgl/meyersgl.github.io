## Chapter 13: Civil Rights & Equal Protection

*Funding role: most of this chapter changes legal rights and costs little. Civil rights enforcement is a core government function, funded within capped and scored base appropriations rather than placed in the phase-in order. The expanded Disabled Access Credit is paid from the General Fund in tier 1 (Section 27.9). Home- and community-based services are paid through the Universal Health Insurance Trust Fund (decision 0024).*

### 13.1 Guiding Principle

Every person possesses inherent worth and deserves to be treated with dignity, fairness, and respect. Public institutions should safeguard individual rights, ensure due process, and maintain equal treatment under the law regardless of income, race, religion, sex, disability, age, political affiliation, or citizenship status (Governing Principle 1 and the Human Dignity Standard).

Civil rights that depend on one administration's willingness to enforce them can be withdrawn by the next. This chapter therefore writes its protections into statute with clear definitions. It lets the people harmed enforce them in court, and it builds remedies around the limits the Supreme Court has set rather than against them.

### 13.2 Voting Rights Act Restoration \[Statute – High Resistance; Constitutionally Contested\]

In *Louisiana v. Callais* (2026) the Supreme Court held that Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act is violated in redistricting only when the evidence supports a strong inference that a state intentionally drew districts to give minority voters less opportunity because of their race. It also held that challengers must separate race from partisan politics \[1\]. The Court grounded that reading in the Fifteenth Amendment, so a statute cannot simply restore the earlier test. This section strengthens what Congress can strengthen and relies on race-neutral remedies where the Court has narrowed race-conscious ones.

#### 13.2.1 Private Enforcement

Any person, organization, or tribal government harmed by a violation of the Voting Rights Act may sue to enforce it, in its own right or under 42 U.S.C. § 1983. Prevailing plaintiffs shall recover reasonable attorney fees and costs. State attorneys general may also sue. These rights shall not depend on whether the Department of Justice chooses to act.

#### 13.2.2 Vote Denial

A voting qualification, prerequisite, standard, practice, or procedure violates Section 2 if it results in members of a protected group having less opportunity than other voters to register, cast a ballot, or have it counted. Courts shall weigh fixed statutory factors:

- the size of the burden;
- how far the burden falls more heavily on members of the protected group;
- whether less burdensome alternatives would serve the state's stated interest equally well;
- whether the state's stated interest is supported by evidence.

This test replaces the narrower factors in *Brnovich v. Democratic National Committee* (2021). Remedies for vote denial, such as polling places, registration access, and fair list maintenance, do not assign voters by race.

#### 13.2.3 Vote Dilution: Evidence of Intent and Race-Neutral Remedies

For claims that a map dilutes minority votes, the statute shall adopt the intent standard the Court set in *Callais*, and shall set out how intent may be shown. Courts shall treat the following as evidence that supports an inference of intentional discrimination:

- departures from a state's normal redistricting procedures;
- maps adopted mid-decade without a court order;
- refusal to adopt an available alternative map that meets the state's stated goals;
- a recent history of adjudicated voting discrimination in the jurisdiction.

Congress shall compile a detailed legislative record of current discrimination in voting to support these provisions.

Where a violation is found, courts shall prefer race-neutral remedies:

- **Independent redistricting:** congressional and state legislative districts drawn by independent redistricting commissions.
- **No mid-decade maps:** congressional redistricting between censuses is barred except under court order.
- **Multi-member districts:** where single-member districts cannot remedy the violation, multi-member districts elected by ranked-choice voting (Section 12.4).

#### 13.2.4 Known-Practices Preclearance

Before putting into effect a listed high-risk change, every state and local jurisdiction shall give public notice and obtain approval from the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia or the Attorney General. Listed changes are:

- closing or moving polling places;
- removing voters from the rolls outside regular list maintenance;
- changing voter identification rules;
- redistricting mid-decade;
- converting districts to at-large seats.

Any affected voter or organization may challenge an approval in court. Because the list is a list of practices rather than a coverage formula, it does not rely on the kind of formula *Shelby County v. Holder* (2013) struck down.

#### 13.2.5 Timely Relief

- **Expedited courts:** voting cases shall be heard by expedited three-judge district courts, with fixed deadlines for decisions and appeals.
- **Timing rule:** within 60 days of a federal election, courts shall presume that orders changing election procedures are too late. That presumption shall not apply to orders blocking practices already found unlawful.
- **Next-election remedy:** if relief comes too late for one election, it shall take effect automatically for the next. A jurisdiction may not use a practice in a later election once a court has found it unlawful.

These rules govern how federal courts grant relief. They remove the reward for delaying a case until an election is too close to change the rules.

#### 13.2.6 Long-Term Constitutional Protection

If courts narrow these statutory protections further, the platform will consider a constitutional amendment establishing an affirmative individual right to vote (Appendix A).

### 13.3 Equal Treatment Across Civil Rights Laws \[Statute – High Resistance\]

#### 13.3.1 Protected Traits

Every federal civil rights statute covered by Section 13.3.2 shall protect each of the following as a separate, explicitly listed trait:

- race;
- color;
- national origin;
- religion;
- sex;
- sexual orientation;
- gender identity and expression;
- pregnancy, childbirth, and related conditions;
- sex characteristics;
- age;
- disability.

Existing protections, such as for genetic information, remain.

#### 13.3.2 Covered Areas

The protections apply in:

- employment;
- housing;
- credit;
- public accommodations;
- programs that receive federal funds;
- health care.

"Public accommodations" includes retail stores, online services, transportation, and providers of health care and other services offered to the public.

#### 13.3.3 Definitions

- **Sex:** a person's biological sex.
- **Sex characteristics:** physical traits related to sex, including intersex traits.
- **Sexual orientation:** homosexuality, heterosexuality, or bisexuality.
- **Gender identity and expression:** a person's gender-related identity and appearance or behavior, whether or not they match the sex assigned at birth.
- **Pregnancy:** pregnancy, childbirth, lactation, and related medical conditions.
- **Perception and association:** discrimination based on a person's perceived trait, or on their association with someone who has the trait, is covered.
- **Combined traits:** discrimination based on a combination of protected traits is covered even if it would not be shown for each trait alone.
- **Any harm:** a discriminatory action that changes the terms or conditions of employment is unlawful without proof of significant harm, as the Court held in *Muldrow v. City of St. Louis* (2024).
- **Age claims:** an age-discrimination claim succeeds if age was a motivating factor, reversing *Gross v. FBL Financial Services* (2009).

Sex and gender identity are listed and defined separately so that each is protected on its own terms. This writes into the statute, rather than leaving to interpretation, the protection *Bostock v. Clayton County* (2020) recognized in employment.

Because courts no longer defer to agencies' readings of unclear statutes, these definitions shall control. No agency rule or guidance may narrow them.

#### 13.3.4 Disparate Impact

A practice that causes a disparate impact on a protected trait shall be unlawful, under every statute in Section 13.3.2, unless the covered entity shows it is job-related or necessary to achieve a substantial, legitimate interest. Even then, it remains unlawful if a less discriminatory alternative would serve that interest equally well. This is the three-step framework of the Civil Rights Act of 1991, extended to every covered area.

People harmed by disparate impact in federally funded programs may sue, reversing *Alexander v. Sandoval* (2001). This section supersedes Executive Order 14281 and the December 2025 Department of Justice rule removing disparate-impact liability from Title VI regulations \[2\].

#### 13.3.5 Health Care

Health care providers and insurers may not refuse care or coverage of medically necessary services because of any protected trait. Standards of care, including for minors, shall be set through the evidence-based benefit process of Universal Health Insurance (Section 5.1), not by statute.

### 13.4 Religious Liberty and Civil Rights \[Statute – High Resistance; Constitutionally Contested\]

The platform protects religious exercise and protects people from discrimination, and it draws the line between them in statute rather than leaving it to case-by-case litigation.

#### 13.4.1 Houses of Worship and the Ministerial Exception

Nothing in this chapter applies to:

- a house of worship's decisions about worship, doctrine, membership, or clergy;
- the constitutional ministerial exception for employees who perform religious functions.

#### 13.4.2 Bona Fide Religious Nonprofits

A bona fide religious nonprofit is a nonprofit organization whose primary purpose is religious and which holds itself out as religious. It may continue to prefer members of its own faith in employment, as current law allows. It may not otherwise discriminate based on a protected trait, except in ministerial positions.

When a religious nonprofit provides services paid for by the government, it shall serve every eligible person without discrimination.

#### 13.4.3 For-Profit Corporations

A for-profit corporation does not exercise religion. A for-profit corporation shall not be a "person" entitled to claim an exemption under the Religious Freedom Restoration Act, and shall not claim any religious exemption from federal anti-discrimination law when operating in the commercial marketplace. This reverses *Burwell v. Hobby Lobby Stores* (2014) by statute.

#### 13.4.4 Limits on the Religious Freedom Restoration Act

The Religious Freedom Restoration Act shall not provide a claim or defense in:

- any case concerning the sale of goods or services in the commercial marketplace;
- emergency medical care;
- services paid for by the government.

#### 13.4.5 Expressive Work

The First Amendment protects against being compelled to create a custom message one does not hold, as the Court held in *303 Creative LLC v. Elenis* (2023). This chapter does not require that. But the sale of ordinary goods and services, which do not themselves express a message, is not expressive work, and a business that sells them to the public must sell them to everyone.

### 13.5 Disability Rights Modernization \[Statute\]

#### 13.5.1 Digital Accessibility

Websites, mobile applications, and digital services of state and local governments and of businesses open to the public shall meet Web Content Accessibility Guidelines 2.1, Level AA. The Access Board shall update the standard by rule as the guidelines advance.

- **Deadlines:** compliance deadlines shall be fixed in statute, with longer phase-in periods for small businesses.
- **Time to fix:** a business that receives written notice of a barrier shall have 90 days to fix it before a lawsuit may seek damages. This keeps the focus on access rather than on suits filed mainly to collect fees.
- **Tax credit:** the Disabled Access Credit for small businesses shall be expanded to cover digital accessibility and raised to help them meet the standard. It is funded from the General Fund in tier 1 (Section 27.9).

The deadlines for the 2024 Title II rule have already been delayed once by interim rule \[3\]. Writing the standard and deadlines into statute prevents further delay.

#### 13.5.2 Automated Decisions

Automated and AI systems used to make or support decisions about hiring, employment, housing, credit, public benefits, or health care shall be accessible to people with disabilities. Anyone affected may request review by a person instead, and that request shall itself be an accommodation the decision-maker must provide. This section works alongside the AI oversight framework in Chapter 19 and the workplace rules in Section 3.6.

#### 13.5.3 Community Integration

The Supreme Court's decision in *Olmstead v. L.C.* (1999) shall be written into statute. People with disabilities have the right to receive services in the most integrated setting appropriate to their needs, and public entities shall not rely on institutions where community services would serve. The Money Follows the Person program shall be made permanent.

Home- and community-based services shall become a guaranteed benefit of Universal Health Insurance, ending the bias toward institutional care and the waiting lists for community services. This benefit shall be designed together with the Universal Health Insurance long-term care benefit, which remains an open question in Section 5.1.7, and with the long-term care relief offered to states in the Universal Health Insurance Partnership Compact (Section 4.7.3).

#### 13.5.4 Workplace Accommodations

- **Documentation:** no medical documentation shall be required when a disability or the need for an accommodation is obvious or already known. A permanent condition shall need to be documented only once.
- **Response time:** employers shall respond to accommodation requests within 15 business days.
- **Remote work:** remote work shall be presumed a reasonable accommodation for a job the employee has already performed remotely without problems.
- **Consistency:** accommodation rules under the Americans with Disabilities Act and the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act shall follow the same procedures.

#### 13.5.5 Ending Subminimum Wages

New certificates allowing employers to pay workers with disabilities less than the minimum wage, under Section 14(c) of the Fair Labor Standards Act, shall end on enactment. Existing certificates shall be phased out over five years. Transition grants shall help providers and workers move to competitive, integrated employment.

### 13.6 Pay Transparency & Equal Pay \[Statute\]

#### 13.6.1 Pay Data

Each employer with 100 or more employees shall report each year the number of employees in each pay band, by sex and by race and ethnicity.

- **Who collects it:** the Census Bureau or the Bureau of Labor Statistics, under federal statistical confidentiality protections.
- **Publication:** aggregate results by industry, occupation, and region shall be published each year.
- **Compelling collection:** collection is required by statute and is not left to the agency's discretion. Any person may sue to compel it if it is withheld. The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission has proposed ending the demographic reporting employers have filed for nearly 60 years \[4\], so the duty is written into law.

#### 13.6.2 Hiring Transparency

Employers with 15 or more employees:

- may not ask job applicants about their pay history or rely on it to set pay;
- shall include a good-faith pay range in every job posting.

#### 13.6.3 Proving Pay Discrimination

- **Justifying pay differences:** a pay difference between employees doing equal work is lawful only if the employer shows it is based on a genuine factor, such as education, training, or experience, that is related to the job and consistent with business necessity.
- **Group cases:** employees may bring group cases under the Equal Pay Act on an opt-out basis, meaning affected employees are included unless they choose to leave.
- **Pay discussions:** employees may not be retaliated against for discussing or disclosing their pay.
- **Statistical gaps:** when pay data show an unexplained pay gap on a protected trait, the burden shifts to the employer to show the gap rests on lawful factors.
- **Race and national origin:** the Equal Pay Act's protections shall extend to race and national origin, not only sex.

### 13.7 Enforcement That Endures \[Statute\]

Under *Trump v. Slaughter* (2026), Congress cannot protect the heads of agencies such as the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission from removal. This chapter therefore does not depend on those agencies acting. Its rights are enforceable by the people they protect (Appendix C).

#### 13.7.1 Private Enforcement and State Attorneys General

Every statute in this chapter shall allow private lawsuits, with recovery of reasonable attorney fees for prevailing plaintiffs. State attorneys general may also enforce it.

#### 13.7.2 Right to Sue When an Agency Does Not Act

If the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission or another agency has not resolved a discrimination charge within 180 days, the person who filed it may go directly to court.

#### 13.7.3 No Forced Arbitration

Agreements made before a dispute arises that require arbitration of civil rights claims, or that waive group or class actions, shall be unenforceable. This extends the Ending Forced Arbitration of Sexual Assault and Sexual Harassment Act of 2022 to every civil rights claim.

#### 13.7.4 Commission on Civil Rights in the Legislative Branch

The U.S. Commission on Civil Rights shall become a legislative-branch commission that investigates and reports to Congress. Its members shall be appointed by congressional leaders of both parties. It shall publish an annual report on enforcement by each federal agency, including cases dropped and the reasons given.

#### 13.7.5 Career Staff and Reasoned Decisions

Career civil rights attorneys and investigators shall keep civil-service protections. When the Department of Justice abandons a pending civil rights case or a consent decree, it shall publish written reasons and notify Congress.

### 13.9 Honest Limits

- **The Supreme Court has set limits a statute cannot cross.** *Callais* rests on the Constitution, so Section 13.2 cannot restore the earlier redistricting test. Its race-neutral remedies and evidentiary rules are the strongest durable response available, but they will be tested in court, and some justices would hold that Section 2 does not apply to redistricting at all \[1\]. The timing rule in Section 13.2.5 limits a judge-made doctrine, and courts may resist it.
- **Disparate impact is under constitutional pressure.** Some justices have questioned whether disparate-impact liability is consistent with equal protection. A statute is far more durable than a regulation, but it is not beyond challenge.
- **Religious liberty claims will be litigated.** Congress can amend the Religious Freedom Restoration Act, but it cannot amend the First Amendment.
  - Whether for-profit corporations have constitutional free-exercise rights has not been settled.
  - Courts have held that laws with broad secular exemptions may not be "generally applicable" under the Free Exercise Clause. Size thresholds such as the 15-employee rule could be used to argue that point.
  - The protection for expressive work in *303 Creative* applies to businesses as well as individuals, because the plaintiff there was a business. This chapter can limit what counts as expressive, but it cannot limit who may claim that protection.
- **The scope of protected traits is contested.** Listing sex, sexual orientation, and gender identity as separate traits will draw opposition, especially in health care, sports, and single-sex facilities. This chapter leaves standards of care to the Universal Health Insurance evidence process and does not settle every application in statute. Those questions will be argued in Congress and in court.
- **Digital accessibility costs money.** Small businesses will bear real compliance costs. The 90-day period to fix problems and the expanded tax credit reduce that burden but do not eliminate it.
- **Community services depend on an unfinished benefit.** Home- and community-based services can only be guaranteed once the Universal Health Insurance long-term care benefit is designed (Section 5.1.7).
- **Ending subminimum wages will be resisted.** Some families and providers will oppose the phase-out of Section 14(c), and the transition must be managed so that no worker loses a job without a better option.
- **Employers will oppose the pay and arbitration rules.** The arbitration ban, opt-out group cases, and burden-shifting rules will meet strong employer opposition.
- **Private enforcement depends on access to lawyers.** Fee-shifting makes it possible, but people in places with few civil rights lawyers will still be underserved.
- **Legislative-branch commissions have limits.** The Commission on Civil Rights can investigate and report, but it cannot enforce. Enforcement comes from courts, private parties, and state attorneys general.
