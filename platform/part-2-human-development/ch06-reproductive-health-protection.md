## Chapter 6: Reproductive Health Protection

### 6.1 Reproductive Health Protection \[Statute – High Resistance\]

Federal law shall protect access to reproductive healthcare, including contraception, abortion care, and pregnancy and postpartum care, nationwide.

Reproductive and maternal health services, including contraception, abortion care, and pregnancy and postpartum care, shall be covered under the public insurance system in Section 5.1.

#### 6.1.1 Abortion Care Is Health Care

Abortion care is women's health care. It shall be legal nationwide so that women can get the medical care they need.

The decision whether to have an abortion belongs to the woman and her doctor. Government shall not substitute its judgment for theirs, and medical decisions shall be guided by clinical standards of care, not by political interference.

#### 6.1.2 Federal Protection of Access

Federal law shall guarantee the legal right to abortion nationwide and shall preempt state laws that ban abortion or impose restrictions that are not medically necessary. Access to medication abortion, including by telehealth, shall be protected from restrictions that are not supported by medical evidence.

#### 6.1.3 Protection for Patients and Providers

Doctors and other clinicians shall be free to provide abortion care according to their medical judgment without threat of criminal or civil penalty. Patients shall not be criminalized for their pregnancy outcomes. Patients, providers, and those who assist them shall be protected from penalties imposed by another state for care that is lawful where it is provided.

#### 6.1.4 Coverage and Access

Abortion care shall be covered under the public insurance system in Section 5.1 on the terms set in Section 6.2, and federal policy shall support access in areas with few providers.

### 6.2 Public Coverage of Abortion Care \[Statute – High Resistance\]

*Funding role: abortion care, and the temporary federal share of Medicaid abortion claims, are paid only through the Universal Health Insurance Trust Fund. Nothing in this chapter draws on the General Fund (decision 0026).*

Since 1976, the Hyde Amendment and related riders on annual spending bills have barred federal coverage of most abortions. Under Universal Health Insurance, nearly everyone's coverage is federal. Applied to Universal Health Insurance, a Hyde-style exclusion would therefore remove abortion coverage for almost the whole country, not only for people on Medicaid. This section sets coverage rules in the Universal Health Insurance statute itself. They are clear enough that no patient loses coverage because she did not know the deadline.

#### 6.2.1 Coverage Through 24 Weeks

Universal Health Insurance shall cover abortion care through 24 weeks and 6 days of pregnancy, measured from the first day of the last menstrual period, for any reason. Every patient shall be told this date in writing at her first pregnancy-related visit.

**Why 24 weeks and 6 days.** The date was chosen for six reasons:

1. **A fixed date, not "viability."** Medicine recognizes no single clinical definition of viability by gestational age. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists describes a "periviable" range of roughly 20 to 25 weeks, and the prospects of an infant born early depend on the individual pregnancy and the care available \[1\]. A coverage rule tied to viability would require a judgment about each pregnancy, and a patient could not know her deadline in advance. A fixed date can be known by every patient and clinician from the first visit.
2. **Time after the anatomy scan.** Most serious fetal conditions are found at the routine anatomy ultrasound, performed between 18 and 22 weeks \[3\]. Scans before 20 weeks are often incomplete, and a substantial share of anomalies are identified only at a follow-up scan \[4\]. Families who learn of a serious diagnosis at 20 to 22 weeks need time for a repeat scan, specialist confirmation, and counseling. An earlier date, such as 15 or 20 weeks, would end coverage for exactly those families.
3. **Not later.** By 25 to 26 weeks most infants born early survive with intensive care. Covering abortion for any reason past that point would not be defensible.
4. **A familiar reference point.** Twenty-four weeks has been the most common reference point for viability in U.S. law since *Roe v. Wade* (1973), so the platform does not invent a new line.
5. **Allowance for dating error.** The additional six days allow for the margin of error in dating a pregnancy during the second trimester, so that no patient loses coverage over a disagreement about dating.
6. **Few patients are affected.** In 2022, 92.8 percent of abortions took place at or before 13 weeks of pregnancy and 1.1 percent at 21 weeks or later \[5\]. The date matters mainly for patients with a late diagnosis, a serious complication, or a pregnancy caused by abuse, and Sections 6.2.2 to 6.2.4 are written for them.

#### 6.2.2 Coverage After 24 Weeks

After 24 weeks and 6 days, Universal Health Insurance shall cover abortion care when, in the reasonable medical judgment of the treating clinician:

- it is necessary to protect the patient's life or physical or mental health;
- the fetus has a condition that is incompatible with life outside the uterus or that would cause death shortly after birth; or
- the pregnancy resulted from rape or incest (Section 6.2.3).

No second opinion, committee approval, or government review shall be required before covered care is provided.

#### 6.2.3 Survivors of Rape and Incest

Abortion care for a pregnancy that resulted from rape or incest shall be covered at any point in pregnancy at which the care is lawful.

- **No reporting requirement:** coverage shall not depend on a police report or a court finding.
- **Clinician certification:** the treating clinician shall certify in the medical record that the patient disclosed that the pregnancy resulted from rape or incest. That certification is sufficient for coverage.
- **Other documentation accepted:** documentation from any one of the following is also sufficient, but none is required:
  - a report to law enforcement or child protective services;
  - a rape crisis center or victim advocate;
  - a school counselor;
  - a forensic medical examination;
  - a protective order.
- **Confidentiality:** the statement shall be confidential health information and shall not be shared with law enforcement without the patient's consent, except as required to protect a child from ongoing abuse.

- **Annual reporting:** Universal Health Insurance shall publish each year the de-identified number of claims paid under this section, by stage of pregnancy.

Survivors, especially minors and victims of incest, often recognize or disclose a pregnancy late. Coverage should not end because of delays the abuse itself caused.

This approach builds on long-standing federal Medicaid policy under the Hyde Amendment. That policy has never required a police report. It allows states only reasonable documentation requirements that may not deny or impede coverage, and it accepts a physician's certification when the patient was unable to report \[6\].

#### 6.2.4 No One Runs Out of Time

- **Request-date rule:** coverage shall be judged by the date the patient first asks a covered provider for abortion care. If that date is within the period in Section 6.2.1, the care shall be covered even if scheduling, travel, transfer, or provider availability delays it past 24 weeks and 6 days.
- **Early diagnosis:** pregnancy tests, ultrasounds to date the pregnancy, and options counseling shall be covered with no cost to the patient and no waiting period.
- **No coverage delays:** Universal Health Insurance shall not impose waiting periods, prior authorization, or mandatory counseling as conditions of coverage for abortion care.
- **Notice of the date:** every patient who receives a positive pregnancy test from a covered provider shall be given, in writing, the date her pregnancy reaches 24 weeks and 6 days, and the Universal Health Insurance directory of providers (Section 6.3.2).

#### 6.2.5 Ending the Hyde Riders

The Hyde Amendment shall not apply to Universal Health Insurance or to Medicaid. Federal health coverage provided through federal employees' health plans, the District of Columbia, TRICARE, the Indian Health Service, federal prisons, and the Peace Corps shall cover abortion care on the same terms as Section 6.2.1 through Section 6.2.4.

The Helms Amendment, which governs foreign assistance, is addressed in Part VIII.

#### 6.2.6 Protection from Annual Spending Riders

Universal Health Insurance benefits shall be paid from the Trust Fund under a permanent appropriation, not through annual spending bills. Removing or restricting a covered benefit shall require amending the Universal Health Insurance statute itself.

#### 6.2.7 Coverage Before Universal Health Insurance Reaches Everyone

Until Universal Health Insurance covers every resident, Medicaid shall cover abortion care on the terms of Sections 6.2.1 through 6.2.4. The federal government shall pay 100 percent of the cost of those claims, charged to the Universal Health Insurance Trust Fund as part of the Medicaid transition (Section 27.4). States therefore shall not pay for care they object to, and no existing state funding is put at risk.

Medicaid patients shall have a statutory right to choose any qualified provider willing to treat them, and may sue to enforce that right.

#### 6.2.8 What This Section Does and Does Not Do

Section 6.2 sets what Universal Health Insurance pays for. It does not change the legal protections in Section 6.1. The coverage standard in Section 6.2 does not create any criminal, civil, or regulatory prohibition on abortion after 24 weeks and 6 days. It governs eligibility for Universal Health Insurance benefits only. Care after 24 weeks and 6 days that is not covered under Section 6.2.2 remains lawful under federal law where Section 6.1 protects it, and may be paid for privately or by a state.

### 6.3 Conscience, Patient Information, and Emergency Care \[Statute – High Resistance\]

The platform protects clinicians who cannot in conscience take part in abortion or sterilization. It also protects patients' right to know their options and to receive emergency care, and it protects clinicians who provide that care. Each protection comes with duties, so that one person's conscience never leaves another person without care or information.

#### 6.3.1 Individual Clinicians

No individual clinician shall be required to personally perform or assist in an abortion or sterilization if doing so would violate the clinician's religious beliefs or moral convictions. A clinician who objects shall:

- tell the employer in writing, in advance, so the employer can arrange coverage;
- when a patient asks about or needs such care, tell the patient promptly that the care is available elsewhere, and make sure the patient receives the information required by Section 6.3.2 from the employer or from a non-objecting colleague;
- never give a patient false or misleading information about her diagnosis, her options, or where care is available, and never delay or obstruct her care.

The objecting clinician does not have to make the referral personally. That duty belongs to the institution.

#### 6.3.2 Patients' Right to Know Their Options

Every hospital, clinic, and medical practice that takes part in Universal Health Insurance (a "facility") shall meet the following requirements, whether or not it offers abortion care.

- **Who carries the duty:** these duties fall on the facility, not on any individual clinician who objects. A facility shall arrange for the duties to be carried out by staff who do not object, or through the Universal Health Insurance Patient Navigation Service. No clinician who objects under Section 6.3.1 shall be required personally to recommend, refer for, or arrange abortion care.
- **The navigation service:** Universal Health Insurance shall operate a Patient Navigation Service, available by telephone and online, that:
  - maintains the provider directory;
  - gives patients referrals;
  - schedules appointments;
  - arranges transfers.

- **Options information:** when a patient is pregnant, the facility shall ensure she is told accurately about all of her options, including continuing the pregnancy, adoption, and abortion care, in the same visit in which the pregnancy is confirmed or discussed.
- **Universal Health Insurance referral sheet:** when a patient asks for care the facility does not offer, or when it is medically indicated, the facility shall ensure she receives a referral sheet produced by Universal Health Insurance before she leaves, or within one business day for telehealth. The sheet shall be clearly labeled as a publication of Universal Health Insurance. It shall list:
  - the names, addresses, and telephone numbers of at least two of the nearest providers that offer the care, from the Universal Health Insurance directory;
  - the date her pregnancy reaches 24 weeks and 6 days (Section 6.2.4);
  - how to reach the Patient Navigation Service, which will arrange a transfer or appointment.
- **Transfer through Universal Health Insurance:** if the patient asks, the facility shall notify the Patient Navigation Service, or a receiving provider in Universal Health Insurance that offers the care, within one business day. The receiving provider or Universal Health Insurance, not the declining provider, shall arrange and carry out the transfer.
  - The declining provider need not arrange or perform the transfer itself.
  - It shall send the patient's records to the receiving provider within one business day of the request.
  - It shall not delay, discourage, or obstruct the transfer in any way.
  - Universal Health Insurance shall schedule the care promptly. The request-date rule in Section 6.2.4 protects the patient's coverage if scheduling takes longer.
- **No misleading information:** a provider shall not tell a patient that care is unavailable when it is available elsewhere, shall not give false or misleading information about her diagnosis or options, and shall not answer a request for options with a refusal alone.
- **Providers may speak for themselves:** a provider may tell the patient its own views and why it does not offer the care, so long as it also gives her the referral sheet.

These duties are part of the facility's care for its own patient: informed consent and continuity of care when she asks for a service the provider does not offer. They are not a general notice to the public, and the information comes from Universal Health Insurance in its own name. That design is meant to respect the First Amendment limits on compelled speech set out in *National Institute of Family and Life Advocates v. Becerra* (2018) \[7\].
- **Public directory:** Universal Health Insurance shall maintain a public directory of providers offering abortion care, searchable by location and pregnancy stage, from which the referral sheet is generated.

#### 6.3.3 Institutions

- **Religious nonprofits:** a bona fide religious nonprofit hospital or clinic (Section 13.4.2) may decline to offer elective abortion and sterilization if it:
  - publishes the policy prominently, including on its website and at admission;
  - tells patients in writing before scheduled obstetric or gynecologic care;
  - meets every duty in Section 6.3.2;
  - provides all emergency care required by Section 6.3.4.
- **For-profit and public institutions:** for-profit and public hospitals and clinics may not adopt an institutional policy of refusing this care. Individual clinicians who work there keep their protection under Section 6.3.1 (Section 13.4.3).
- **Access where there is no alternative:** where a religious nonprofit is the only hospital within a travel time set in statute, Universal Health Insurance shall ensure access to the care it declines to provide. It may do so through contracts with other providers, telehealth, or arranged and paid transport.
- **Narrowed conscience statutes:** the Weldon and Coats-Snowe Amendments shall be narrowed to match this section.

#### 6.3.4 Emergency Care Is Required

Every hospital that takes part in Universal Health Insurance or Medicare, and every clinician treating a patient there, shall provide stabilizing treatment for an emergency medical condition under the Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act. Stabilizing treatment for a pregnant patient explicitly includes abortion care and the treatment of miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, previable rupture of membranes, preeclampsia, infection, hemorrhage, and similar conditions. It is required whenever, in the treating clinician's reasonable medical judgment, the absence of treatment could reasonably be expected to:

- place the patient's life or health in serious jeopardy;
- cause serious impairment of bodily functions; or
- cause serious dysfunction of any bodily organ.

The patient need not be at imminent risk of death.

This obligation, whose constitutional basis is set out in Section 6.3.8, shall not depend on agency guidance. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services rescinded its 2022 guidance on this point in June 2025 \[2\], so the obligation is written into statute.

- **No institutional exemption:** no hospital may refuse required emergency care on grounds of conscience or religion.
- **Individual clinicians:** an individual clinician may step aside only if another qualified clinician is immediately available to provide the care without delay. Otherwise the clinician shall provide it.

#### 6.3.5 Clinicians Who Provide Emergency Care Are Protected

- **No lawyer before care:** stabilizing treatment shall not be delayed for legal review, ethics committee approval, a second opinion, or consultation with counsel. No hospital policy may require any of these before emergency care.
- **Reasonable medical judgment governs:** a clinician who provides stabilizing treatment under Section 6.3.4 in good-faith, reasonable medical judgment has acted lawfully. The standard is that of a reasonable clinician at the time of treatment, not hindsight.
- **Federal protection from state penalties:** no state or local government shall impose criminal, civil, licensing, or professional penalties on a clinician or hospital for stabilizing treatment provided under Section 6.3.4. Conflicting state laws are preempted.
  - A clinician charged or sued under state law for such care may move the case to federal court and raise this section as a defense.
  - A clinician threatened with such penalties may sue in federal court to stop them.
  - A clinician who prevails recovers reasonable attorney fees.
- **No retaliation:** no hospital, employer, or licensing body may retaliate against a clinician for providing, or for insisting on providing, required emergency care.

#### 6.3.6 Scope of Conscience Protections

Conscience protections under this section apply to taking part in procedures, not to choosing which patients to treat. They cover:

- abortion;
- sterilization;
- medical aid in dying;
- research activities currently protected under federal law.

They do not permit refusing care to a patient because of a protected trait (Section 13.3.5).

A pharmacist may step aside from dispensing contraception or medication for abortion, but the pharmacy shall fill the prescription without delay. It may use another pharmacist on duty or transfer the prescription within the same day at the patient's choice.

#### 6.3.7 Protection in Both Directions and Enforcement

- **Both directions:** no clinician shall face discrimination in employment, training, licensing, or staff privileges because the clinician declines to take part in care covered by Section 6.3.6, or because the clinician provides lawful reproductive care, including in another state (Section 6.1.3).
- **Who may sue:** clinicians, and patients denied the information or care this section requires, may sue to enforce it and recover reasonable attorney fees. State attorneys general and the Department of Health and Human Services may also enforce it.
- **Conditions of participation:** repeated violations of Sections 6.3.2 through 6.3.5 shall also be grounds for civil penalties and, in serious cases, exclusion from Universal Health Insurance.

#### 6.3.8 Constitutional Basis

Congress enacts Sections 6.3.2 through 6.3.5, including the preemption of conflicting state law, under three of its constitutional powers.

- **Federally funded health care:** the power to set conditions on the federal funds that pay for health care. Every covered hospital, clinic, and practice takes part voluntarily in Universal Health Insurance or Medicare, and these duties and protections are conditions of that participation, stated clearly in the statute.
- **Interstate commerce:** the power to regulate interstate commerce. Hospitals, emergency care, health insurance, medical supplies and drugs, and the movement of patients and clinicians across state lines are interstate commerce, and the delay or denial of emergency care burdens that commerce.
- **Supremacy of federal law:** under the Supremacy Clause, these provisions displace any state law that conflicts with them, including state laws that would penalize care this chapter requires or protects.

Each of the first two powers is a separate and sufficient basis. If a court holds that one does not support a provision, the provision shall remain in effect under the other. If any provision is held invalid, the remaining provisions shall continue in effect.

### 6.4 Honest Limits

- **The 24-week date will be attacked from both directions.** Some will say a fixed date is arbitrary, since viability varies, or that it is too early. Others will say Universal Health Insurance should not pay for abortion at all, or that 24 weeks is too late. The platform chose a fixed date so that patients can know it in advance, and Section 6.2.1 sets out why that date was chosen. The strongest criticism is that survival in the 24th week is already substantial with intensive care. The request-date rule and early-diagnosis coverage protect patients who learn of a pregnancy late.
- **Taxpayer objections are sincere and lasting.** Many Americans believe abortion ends a human life and object to public funding. Supporters of the Hyde Amendment credit it with reducing abortions. This chapter does not resolve that disagreement. It sets coverage rules in statute, limits coverage after 24 weeks to defined circumstances, and fully protects clinicians who will not take part.
- **Statutes can be repealed.** Section 6.2.6 makes a new restriction harder to add through a spending bill, but a future Congress can amend the Universal Health Insurance statute. Passing this chapter at all would likely require changing the Senate filibuster, a question for Appendix B.
- **The rape and incest provision does not require a police report.** Critics will say it is open to abuse. The provision answers that in three ways:
  - it requires a clinician's certification;
  - it accepts a range of other documentation;
  - it publishes annual counts of claims.

  The number of cases involved is small: only about 1 percent of abortions occur at 21 weeks or later, and pregnancies from rape or incest after 24 weeks are a small share of those \[5\]. Requiring a police report would deter survivors, especially minors and victims of family members, from seeking care.
- **Emergency-care preemption will be challenged.** The obligation rests on Congress's power to set conditions for hospitals that take federal health funding, and on the Supremacy Clause. In 2024 the Supreme Court dismissed the Idaho case without deciding whether the Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act overrides state abortion bans. The question remains open. Writing the duty and the protections for clinicians into statute is the strongest available answer, but courts will decide it.
- **Duties on religious institutions will be litigated.** Religious hospitals may argue that the options-information and referral duties burden their religious exercise, or compel speech in violation of the First Amendment. *NIFLA v. Becerra* struck down a state requirement that licensed pregnancy centers post a state-written notice about abortion services \[7\]. Section 6.3.2 is designed around that decision in three ways:
  - it ties the duties to the provider's care for its own patient;
  - it uses a referral sheet issued by Universal Health Insurance in its own name;
  - it lets providers state their own views.

  Courts have not decided whether that is enough.
- **Access where only a religious hospital exists depends on resources.** Guaranteeing access in communities whose only hospital is a religious nonprofit requires contracts, telehealth, and transport that Universal Health Insurance must fund and maintain.
