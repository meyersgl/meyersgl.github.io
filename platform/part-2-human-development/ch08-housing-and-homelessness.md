## Chapter 8: Housing & Homelessness

### 8.1 Affordable Housing Initiative \[Statute / Statute – High Resistance\]

Housing affordability represents one of the largest economic challenges facing American families.

Federal policy shall focus on increasing housing supply rather than restricting development.

#### 8.1.1 Upzoning Incentives

Federal transportation and housing funding may be conditioned upon local housing production reforms.

#### 8.1.2 Permitting Modernization

Investments shall support:

- Digital permitting systems

- Staffing expansion

- Pre-approved building designs

- Clear, predictable review timelines achieved through staffing and capacity, without weakening environmental or safety standards

These investments apply the national permitting rule in Section 21.9 to housing.

#### 8.1.3 Institutional Ownership Limits

Federal policy shall restrict excessive concentration of single-family housing within large institutional investment portfolios. Limits shall apply to institutional investors whose holdings meet the Dominance Threshold in a local housing market, measured by their share of single-family homes, and shall target purchasing conduct rather than size alone.

#### 8.1.4 Public Housing Developer

A publicly operated housing developer shall construct and maintain mixed-income housing developments in high-demand markets.

#### 8.1.5 First-Time Homebuyer Assistance

Down payment assistance shall be available expressly to first-time homebuyers. It shall be income-limited and designed (for example, with shared-equity terms) so that it does not simply bid up prices, and it shall be paired with the supply measures above.

#### 8.1.6 Homeowner Protections

Property-tax relief for seniors and low-income homeowners shall ensure that rising land values do not force long-time residents out. Owners shall have a by-right ability to add accessory units or split lots. Housing reforms shall be phased in so that no homeowner's equity is wiped out.

### 8.2 Anti-Gouging Rent Stabilization \[Statute – High Resistance\]

Rent stabilization in this platform is a guard against gouging, not a substitute for building. It limits sudden, large increases on people already living in a home, leaves new buildings free for fifteen years, and lets rent reset between tenants, so that the supply measures in Section 8.1 keep doing the main work. Where landlords are large enough to rely on federal tax benefits and federally backed financing, the federal government sets the floor directly. Elsewhere, it pays states and localities to set and enforce their own caps.

#### 8.2.1 The Cap

For a covered unit (Section 8.2.2), the rent charged to a sitting tenant shall not rise in any 12-month period by more than the annual change in the regional Consumer Price Index plus 5 percentage points, and never by more than 10 percent. Rent may be raised no more than once in any 12-month period. Any fee or charge that a tenant must pay as a condition of living in the unit counts as rent.

#### 8.2.2 Who Is Covered

The federal floor applies to every landlord whose portfolio, counting affiliates and entities under common control, includes 50 or more residential rental units. Splitting ownership among related entities shall not avoid coverage. This unit-count test is separate from the Dominance Threshold used for single-family ownership limits in Section 8.1.3.

The following units are exempt:

- units first occupied within the previous 15 years, measured from the certificate of occupancy;
- units in an owner-occupied building of one to four units;
- units whose rent is already limited by a federal subsidy program, such as the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit or project-based rental assistance, where the subsidy program's own rule is stricter. Where it is less strict, the cap in Section 8.2.1 also applies.

#### 8.2.3 How the Floor Is Applied Through Federal Benefits

The floor is enforced through federal benefits that landlords choose to accept, not through a direct federal order on rents. A covered landlord that raises rent on a unit above the cap:

- shall, for the building containing that unit, compute depreciation under the alternative depreciation system and lose bonus depreciation on its components, for the tax year of the violation and the following tax year;
- shall not defer gain on a sale or exchange of that building under like-kind exchange rules (26 U.S.C. § 1031) during the same period.

Every multifamily loan that Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac purchases or guarantees, or that the Federal Housing Administration insures, shall include compliance with the cap as a loan covenant for covered landlords. The covenant applies to loans made or refinanced after enactment.

This follows the design of a 2024 federal proposal that tied faster depreciation to a 5 percent limit on rent increases \[7\]. Because landlords choose whether to accept these benefits, and the cap compels no physical occupation of their property, the floor is designed to avoid a claim that it takes property without compensation \[9\].

Revenue that results from lost tax benefits shall go to the General Fund and shall not be assigned to any program (Section 27.8).

#### 8.2.4 Protections Within the Cap

- **Vacancy reset.** When a tenant leaves voluntarily, or a tenancy ends for one of the just causes below other than a no-fault reason, the rent may be reset for the next tenant.
- **Just cause for eviction.** A covered landlord may end a tenancy or refuse to renew only for nonpayment of rent, a material breach of the lease, illegal activity or serious nuisance, the owner or an immediate family member moving in, withdrawal of the unit from the rental market, or substantial rehabilitation that requires the unit to be vacant.
- **No-fault moves.** When a tenancy ends for one of the last three reasons, the landlord shall pay relocation assistance equal to three months' rent. For the following two years, the rent for the unit may not exceed what the cap would have allowed for the departing tenant.
- **Fair return.** A landlord whose increases in operating costs (property taxes, insurance, and utilities) and amortized capital improvements cannot be met within the cap while earning a fair return may petition for a larger increase. Petitions shall be decided by administrative law judges within 90 days. If a petition is not decided in time, the landlord may charge the requested increase while it is pending, and must refund it with interest if the petition is denied.
- **Coordinated pricing.** Setting rents through a shared pricing service that pools nonpublic rent and occupancy data from competing landlords shall be treated as an unreasonable restraint of trade under the competition laws (Chapter 9).

#### 8.2.5 Enforcement

- **Tenants:** a tenant charged more than the cap may sue to recover the overcharge, plus triple damages for willful violations and attorney fees. An eviction or nonrenewal brought in retaliation for asserting these rights is void.
- **States:** state attorneys general may enforce this section in federal or state court.
- **Reporting:** each covered landlord shall file an annual rent-roll information return with the Internal Revenue Service showing, for each unit, the rent charged and any increase. The Internal Revenue Service has a mandatory duty to match returns against tax filings and assess the loss of benefits under Section 8.2.3. Any person may sue to compel performance of that duty.
- **No waivers:** no agency may waive or suspend the cap, the coverage rules, or these enforcement duties.

#### 8.2.6 State Stabilization Grants

Grants shall be available to states and localities that adopt caps no weaker than Section 8.2.1, apply them to smaller landlords not covered by the federal floor, and fund enforcement, including a rent registry and legal help for tenants. This section does not preempt stronger state or local protections.

### 8.3 Ending Homelessness \[Statute – High Resistance\]

On a single night in January 2025, 745,652 people were homeless in the United States, the first decline in nearly a decade. Chronic homelessness, however, reached its highest level on record, about 155,750 people \[1\]. Federal policy has since moved toward treatment-first conditions and civil commitment (Executive Order 14321), and HUD has tried twice to cap the share of Continuum of Care funds available for permanent housing. The first attempt was enjoined and vacated, and the second remains in litigation \[2\]\[3\].

This section sets a different course. A home comes first because the evidence on housing stability is strong. Recovery and treatment are offered as real choices, not as conditions for getting a home. And the rules are written in statute so that they cannot be reversed by a funding notice.

#### 8.3.1 Housing First as the Federal Standard

- **No preconditions.** Federally funded permanent supportive housing and rapid rehousing shall offer housing without requiring sobriety, participation in treatment, employment, or income. Services shall be offered and voluntary. A tenancy may be ended only for the same lease violations that apply to any other tenant.
- **Supportive services.** Permanent supportive housing projects shall maintain access to multidisciplinary support teams, which may include mental-health clinicians, substance-use counselors, primary-care providers, peer-support specialists, case managers, employment specialists, and benefits navigators. Services shall be offered proactively, on site where feasible, and shall remain voluntary. Refusing services shall never be grounds for eviction, loss of housing, reduced tenant rights, or reduced benefits. Clinical services are paid by Universal Health Insurance (Sections 5.1.1 and 8.3.5).
- **Permanent housing floor.** At least 70 percent of Continuum of Care funds each year shall go to permanent housing, including permanent supportive housing and rapid rehousing.
- **Renewal.** Projects that meet published performance standards shall be renewed.
- **Predictable competitions.** HUD shall publish each year's funding notice by a deadline set in statute. If it does not, the prior year's terms renew automatically. Any change to scoring or eligibility criteria requires notice and comment and a published statement of reasons.
- **Coordination.** The United States Interagency Council on Homelessness shall be continued in statute, with a mandatory duty to publish a national plan and an annual progress report.

#### 8.3.2 Recovery Housing as a Choice

Federal funds shall support recovery housing that requires abstinence, provided it meets national quality standards. A person may choose recovery housing, but it shall never be the only offer made. A resident who leaves recovery housing or relapses shall not lose housing assistance and shall be offered a transfer to housing under Section 8.3.1.

#### 8.3.3 Housing Vouchers

- **Guarantee.** Every extremely low-income household that is experiencing homelessness or at imminent risk of homelessness, as defined in the McKinney-Vento Homeless Assistance Act, shall be entitled to a Housing Choice Voucher once this guarantee takes effect under Section 27.9.
- **Universal stage.** The guarantee shall then extend, in stages, to every extremely low-income renter household paying more than half its income in rent. Each stage takes effect only when funded under Section 27.9, and none before every earlier tier is fully funded.
- **Source of income.** Landlords shall not refuse a tenant because the tenant pays rent with a voucher or other lawful assistance. This protection is enforced with the remedies in Section 13.7.

#### 8.3.4 Encampments and Public Space

*City of Grants Pass v. Johnson* (2024) held that the Eighth Amendment does not bar cities from punishing people for camping in public even when no shelter is available \[8\]. Congress may still set conditions on federal homelessness funds, so long as they are unambiguous, related to the purpose of the funds, and not coercive \[9\]. A state or locality that receives Continuum of Care, Emergency Solutions Grant, or voucher funds:

- may enforce rules against sleeping or sheltering in public only after making the person an **adequate offer**: a non-congregate shelter bed or housing placement available that day, accessible to the person's disabilities under the reasonable-modification duties of the Americans with Disabilities Act and Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act (as strengthened by Section 13.5), and reasonably accommodating partners, pets, and possessions;
- shall not impose criminal penalties for sleeping, sitting, lying down, or sheltering from the weather in public, and shall not convert unpaid civil citations into warrants or jail;
- shall store belongings it removes for at least 90 days, and shall never destroy identification documents, medication, or medical equipment.

**Safety exceptions.** A locality may clear a site without an adequate offer when it blocks a building entrance or an accessible sidewalk route, lies within a set distance of a school or playground, or presents an imminent threat to health or safety. Even then, the ban on criminal penalties and the storage rules apply.

**Enforcement.** Affected individuals may sue for injunctive relief, damages, and attorney fees. State attorneys general may enforce. HUD has a mandatory duty to recover funds from a grantee found in violation.

#### 8.3.5 Crisis Care and Civil Commitment

- **Crisis care.** The 988 crisis line, mobile crisis teams staffed by clinicians rather than police, and crisis stabilization centers shall be available nationwide. Clinical crisis services are covered mental-health and substance-use services under Universal Health Insurance (Section 5.1.1).
- **Commitment floor.** Any civil commitment carried out in, or paid for by, a federally funded program or facility, including services paid for by Universal Health Insurance, shall meet these minimum standards:
  - homelessness, by itself, is never grounds for commitment;
  - commitment requires that the person, because of mental illness, is a danger to themselves or others or is unable to provide for their basic needs, proven by clear and convincing evidence \[10\];
  - the person has a right to counsel and a prompt hearing within a deadline set in statute;
  - care is provided in the least restrictive appropriate setting, with periodic review;
  - no one is discharged to the street; discharge plans include a housing placement.
- **Outpatient treatment.** Court-ordered outpatient treatment may be funded only with the same protections.
- **Health privacy.** Behavioral health records and homelessness service records shall not be shared with law enforcement without a warrant or court order, except to prevent an imminent threat to life.

This section replaces the civil-commitment approach of Executive Order 14321.

#### 8.3.6 Prevention

- **Emergency rental assistance.** A permanent program shall pay rent arrears and short-term rent for households below 80 percent of area median income facing eviction because of a financial hardship, with payment made directly to the landlord where the tenant agrees. The program has a base amount and rises automatically, within one capped amount, when the recession trigger in Section 1.2 is met.
- **Right to counsel.** Grants shall fund legal representation in eviction cases for tenants with incomes below 200 percent of the poverty line, phased in over five years and starting where eviction filings are highest.
- **Eviction records.** Eviction cases shall be sealed when filed, unsealed only if the court enters judgment for the landlord, and sealed again three years after judgment. Tenant screening companies shall not report sealed cases, enforced through the Fair Credit Reporting Act.

#### 8.3.7 Durability

These rules follow the design standards in Appendix C. Floors, deadlines, and renewal rules are fixed in statute; changes to criteria require public reasons; individuals and state attorneys general can enforce the duties in court; and the Government Accountability Office reviews the programs under Section 14.2.1.

### 8.5 Honest Limits

- **Rent caps reduce supply at the margin.** The strongest study of rent control, from San Francisco, found that covered landlords cut rental supply by 15 percent, raising rents citywide by about 5 percent \[6\]. This chapter's protections are the 15-year exemption for new buildings, vacancy reset, fair-return petitions, and the supply measures in Section 8.1. They reduce the risk; they do not remove it.
- **Landlords can give up the benefits instead.** A landlord with little depreciation left and no federally backed loan may simply accept the tax consequences and raise rents. State stabilization grants are meant to fill that gap, but some states will not take them.
- **Fair-return petitions take work.** They are needed for the cap to be fair and legally durable, but they will add caseload for administrative law judges.
- **Housing First has not by itself reduced chronic homelessness.** The evidence that it keeps people housed is strong, but its effects on health are mixed \[4\]\[5\], and chronic homelessness rose to a record while it was federal policy. This chapter's view is that the shortage of affordable homes and vouchers is the binding constraint. That is an argument, not a proven result.
- **Some cities will refuse the money.** A city that values its camping ban more than its federal homelessness grants can decline them, and the encampment conditions will not reach it.
- **The commitment floor will be criticized.** Some families of people with serious mental illness, and some city officials, want broader authority to commit. This chapter accepts that cost to protect people who have committed no crime.
- **Vouchers without supply can raise rents.** Expanding vouchers in tight markets can push rents up unless more homes are built, which is why the universal stage comes last.
- **Nothing in this chapter has been scored,** and the legal fight over Continuum of Care funding is still under way, so the starting point will keep shifting.
