## Chapter 7: Education

*Funding role: universal preschool is paid from the General Fund in tier 2. K-12 equity funding, tuition-free public higher education, and existing student-loan reform are paid from the General Fund in tier 3, in the order set by Section 27.9, and may also be paid from the wealth-tax transfer in Section 27.5. Implementation order is governed by Section 27.9.*

### 7.1 Universal Preschool \[Statute\]

Every four-year-old child shall have access to publicly funded preschool education.

The goals are:

- Early childhood development

- School readiness

- Workforce participation

- Reduced long-term educational inequality

### 7.2 K-12 Educational Opportunity Framework \[Statute\]

Every child shall have access to a high-quality public education regardless of zip code, local property values, or family income. Educational opportunity shall be a national commitment rather than a function of local wealth.

#### 7.2.1 Equal Opportunity Funding

Federal and state education funding shall reduce disparities created by local property-tax dependence. Public schools serving low-income communities shall receive sufficient resources to provide educational opportunities substantially equivalent to those available in wealthier districts. Federal funding shall be weighted toward high-poverty districts and tied to state equity-of-funding requirements.

#### 7.2.2 National Education Standards

States shall retain control over curriculum while ensuring students receive adequate instruction in mathematics, science, literacy, civics, history, technology, and critical thinking. Minimum standards in these subjects shall be developed cooperatively with the states and tied to federal funding.

### 7.3 Tuition-Free Public Higher Education \[Statute\]

Tuition shall be fully covered at:

- Public universities

- Community colleges

- Trade schools

- Technical institutions

Federal assistance currently tied to tuition costs should instead be redirected toward:

- Housing

- Food

- Books

- Transportation

- Living expenses

for qualifying students.

#### 7.3.1 Existing Student Loan Reform \[Statute\]

Existing federal student loans shall be refinanced at a near-zero administrative interest rate of 0.01%.

Borrowers making qualifying payments for twenty years (240 monthly payments) shall be eligible for full debt discharge.

Additional penalties, fees, and capitalization of unpaid interest shall be prohibited.

Cost estimates for this provision shall be published under Chapter 27 before implementation.

### 7.4 STEM Education Pipeline \[Statute\]

Federal programs shall expand pathways into:

- Science

- Engineering

- Mathematics

- Healthcare

- Technology

Special attention shall be directed toward:

- Rural communities

- Underserved populations

- First-generation students

These programs are funded within existing authorized appropriations, with no new appropriation and no Section 27.9 phase-in obligation (Section 27.4).

### 7.5 Honest Limits

- **Costs are large and uncertain.** Comparable published estimates in Appendix E put universal preschool at about \$100 billion to \$170 billion over ten years, K-12 equity funding at about \$360 billion to \$400 billion, tuition-free public higher education at about \$700 billion to \$800 billion, and student-loan reform at about \$400 billion to \$900 billion, most with low confidence. In the conservative case in Appendix E, preschool, tuition-free college, and student-loan reform wait until revenue grows.
- **The federal-state split for tuition-free college is not set.** Estimates range from the federal government paying about two-thirds of the cost to paying all of it (Appendix E). States may also cut their own higher-education budgets once federal money arrives, so implementing legislation will need a maintenance-of-effort rule.
- **National standards face legal and political limits.** Current federal education law bars federal officials from requiring states to adopt particular academic standards, so Section 7.2.2 requires changing that law, and conditions on federal funds must be clear and not coercive (*National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius*, 2012).
- **Equity funding can be offset.** States and districts can shift their own funds away from high-poverty schools as federal money arrives. Tying funds to state equity-of-funding requirements (Section 7.2.1) is meant to prevent this, but enforcement is hard.
- **Preschool depends on a workforce that does not yet exist at scale.** Serving every four-year-old requires many more qualified teachers, and early-childhood pay is low, so expansion will take years.
- **Free tuition helps some families who would have paid anyway,** and it may draw students from private colleges and strain capacity at public institutions.
- **The student-loan terms will be attacked as unfair to those who repaid.** A 0.01 percent rate and discharge after twenty years are generous. After *Biden v. Nebraska* (2023), only Congress can enact them.
- **STEM programs get no new money.** Section 7.4 operates within existing appropriations (decision 0042), so its reach depends on annual appropriations.
