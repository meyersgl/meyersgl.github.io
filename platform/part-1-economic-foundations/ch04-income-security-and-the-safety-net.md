## Chapter 4: Income Security & the Safety Net

*Funding role: the income-security measures in this chapter are paid from the General Fund (the federal tax fairness measures in Chapter 2) and come first in the General Fund phase-in order (Section 27.9). Unemployment insurance is paid from its own trust funds. Extended benefits in recessions are paid from the Sovereign Wealth Reserve. The Medicaid transition is paid through the Universal Health Insurance Trust Fund (decision 0023).*

A market economy creates wealth, but it does not guarantee that a child grows up fed, that a worker who loses a job can pay rent while finding the next one, or that a person with a disability can live with dignity. A social insurance system should do those things in a way that always rewards work: no family should be worse off for earning another dollar, and no one who cannot reasonably work should lose food or health care over paperwork.

### 4.1 Welfare Cliff Reform \[Statute\]

Benefit phase-outs shall occur gradually so additional work always increases net household income.

#### 4.1.1 Combined Phase-Out Limit

For any household receiving a federally funded means-tested benefit, the combined share of an additional dollar of earnings lost to benefit reductions and federal taxes shall not exceed 50 percent. The calculation shall count federal income and payroll taxes (both the employee and employer shares, as in Section 2.8), the phase-out of the Child Tax Credit and Earned Income Tax Credit, and reductions in federally funded nutrition, housing, childcare (Section 3.4), and disability benefits.

Programs shall phase out in a coordinated order set by the Department of the Treasury and the Department of Health and Human Services, so that two benefits never shrink steeply over the same income range. No working household should lose a larger share of its next dollar than the 60 percent ceiling that applies to top earners (Section 2.8).

#### 4.1.2 One Application

Federal benefit programs shall share one application. With the applicant's consent, an applicant found eligible for one program shall be screened automatically for the others using records the government already holds.

#### 4.1.3 Annual Cliff Report

The Congressional Budget Office shall publish each year the combined phase-out rates faced by representative low- and moderate-income households, and identify any income range where the limit in Section 4.1.1 is exceeded.

### 4.2 Child Tax Credit \[Statute\]

The children who most need the Child Tax Credit receive the least of it today. The credit is $2,200 per child, but only up to $1,700 is refundable, and a family must earn more than $2,500 before receiving any refund, so the poorest children receive a partial credit or none \[1\].

#### 4.2.1 Full Refundability

The Child Tax Credit of $2,200 per child shall be fully refundable, with no minimum earnings requirement, and indexed for inflation. The existing phase-out above $200,000 of income for single filers and $400,000 for married couples filing jointly shall remain.

#### 4.2.2 Young Child Supplement

Each child under age 6 shall qualify for an additional $1,000, for a total of $3,200, indexed for inflation. The early years are when deprivation does the most lasting harm, and when childcare costs make work hardest to sustain.

#### 4.2.3 Monthly Payment Option

Families may choose to receive the credit in monthly payments. A family whose income changes during the year shall not owe back any advance payment received in good faith, except above a high-income threshold.

#### 4.2.4 Eligibility

Each qualifying child shall have a Social Security number. The claiming parent shall either have a Social Security number or be lawfully present in the United States. This restores the credit to children who are U.S. citizens whose parents are lawfully present but do not have a Social Security number, while keeping the credit unavailable to parents without lawful status.

### 4.3 Earned Income Tax Credit for Workers Without Children \[Statute\]

The Earned Income Tax Credit for workers without qualifying children shall be expanded on the model of the 2021 expansion:

- **Age:** eligibility shall begin at age 19, except for full-time students under 24, with no upper age limit.
- **Amount:** the maximum credit shall be about three times its current value.
- **Income range:** the income range over which the credit phases in and out shall be widened so that low-wage, full-time workers qualify.

Final parameters shall be set after independent scoring. This credit is the platform's direct reward for work. It pairs with the Child Tax Credit's full refundability so that the safety net as a whole continues to reward earning more.

### 4.4 Unemployment Insurance Modernization \[Statute\]

Unemployment insurance reaches too few workers and replaces too little of their pay. Only about 28 percent of unemployed people received benefits in 2025, and those who qualified received about 40 percent of their average weekly earnings. State recipiency ranged from 52 percent in Minnesota to 8 percent in Florida \[2\].

#### 4.4.1 Federal Minimum Standards

As a condition of the existing federal unemployment tax credit, each state program shall meet minimum standards:

- **Replacement:** benefits shall replace at least 50 percent of a claimant's prior weekly wages, up to a maximum weekly benefit of at least two-thirds of the state's average weekly wage.
- **Duration:** benefits shall be available for at least 26 weeks.
- **Eligibility:** eligibility shall count the most recent quarter of earnings. Part-time workers seeking part-time work shall be covered, and so shall workers who leave a job for a compelling family reason such as domestic violence or a caregiving emergency.
- **Independent workers:** workers whose income comes mainly from independent contracting or platform work shall be covered through a federal program funded by contributions from the businesses that contract with them.

#### 4.4.2 Self-Financing

The federal unemployment tax wage base, $7,000 since 1983, shall be raised and indexed to wage growth. States shall maintain trust-fund reserves adequate to pay at least one year of benefits at recession levels. Regular unemployment benefits shall be paid entirely from unemployment trust funds and never from the General Fund.

#### 4.4.3 Modern Administration

The Department of Labor shall set national standards for claims systems, identity verification, and fraud prevention, and shall fund their modernization. Eligible claimants shall be paid within 21 days of filing.

#### 4.4.4 Automatic Extended Benefits in Recessions

When the recession trigger in Section 1.2 is met, additional weeks of benefits shall begin automatically in every state, without new legislation. Extended benefits shall be fully federally funded from draws on the Sovereign Wealth Reserve (Section 27.5). They shall end on the schedule set for recovery confirmation in Section 1.2.

### 4.5 Nutrition Assistance (SNAP) \[Statute\]

The 2025 reconciliation law extended SNAP work requirements to adults up to age 64 and, for the first time, made states with payment error rates of 6 percent or more pay a share of benefit costs \[3\]. This section keeps a work expectation for adults who can reasonably work, but ensures that people who cannot work, and people who are working, do not lose food assistance over paperwork.

#### 4.5.1 Work Expectations and Exemptions

Able-bodied adults ages 18 to 54 without exempting circumstances shall be expected to work or participate in education, training, or community service for at least 80 hours a month. Adults ages 55 to 64 shall be exempt. The following shall also be exempt:

- **Disability:** a person with a physical or mental disability that limits their ability to work, shown by any of the following, without waiting for a federal disability determination:
  - a statement from a licensed clinician;
  - a disability rating from the Department of Veterans Affairs;
  - disability status in Medicaid, Universal Health Insurance, or a state program;
  - a pending application for SSI or Social Security Disability Insurance.
- **Caregivers:**
  - a parent or caregiver of a child under age 6;
  - the sole caregiver of a child under age 14;
  - a person who is the primary caregiver for an elderly relative or for a household member with a disability.

#### 4.5.2 Automatic Verification and a Grace Period

States shall verify work activity and exemptions automatically, first through records the government already holds:

- payroll and new-hire data;
- Medicaid and Universal Health Insurance records;
- Department of Veterans Affairs records;
- state disability and caregiving program records.

Where those records do not settle the question, a household may self-attest to work activity or an exemption for 90 days. The grace period shall be renewed once if the missing information is held by a government agency. No household shall lose benefits without written notice, a chance to respond, and review by a caseworker.

#### 4.5.3 Areas Without Enough Jobs

The Secretary of Agriculture shall waive work expectations in areas where unemployment is substantially above the national rate or where there are not enough jobs for the people expected to work.

#### 4.5.4 Federal Funding and State Performance

The federal government shall again pay the full cost of SNAP benefits. The state cost share tied to payment error rates shall be replaced by performance bonuses for states that reduce errors, including errors that underpay eligible households.

### 4.6 Supplemental Security Income and Disability Insurance \[Statute\]

Supplemental Security Income (SSI) is the income floor for older adults and people with disabilities who have little income. For 2026, the maximum federal benefit is $994 a month for an individual, and the asset limit is $2,000 for an individual and $3,000 for a couple \[4\]. The asset limit has not changed since 1989, so it penalizes recipients who save for an emergency.

#### 4.6.1 Asset Limits

The SSI asset limit shall be $10,000 for an individual and $20,000 for a married couple, indexed for inflation. Setting the couple's limit at twice the individual limit ends the penalty on recipients who marry.

#### 4.6.2 Income Rules

The general and earned income exclusions, unchanged since SSI began in 1974, shall be restored to their original value in today's dollars and indexed for inflation, so that recipients keep more of what they earn.

#### 4.6.3 Disability Insurance Earnings Offset

In Social Security Disability Insurance, the cliff at which earning slightly too much ends benefits shall be replaced by a gradual offset: benefits shall fall by $1 for every $2 earned above the threshold. This offset shall be financed by the Social Security trust funds and scored together with the reforms in Chapter 26.

#### 4.6.4 Future Benefit Adequacy

The maximum SSI benefit is well below the federal poverty line. The platform's long-term goal is to raise it to the poverty line. That increase is not counted in this edition's financing and shall be proposed only after independent scoring.

### 4.7 Medicaid and the Transition to Universal Health Insurance \[Statute – High Resistance\]

#### 4.7.1 Coverage Without Work Requirements

The Medicaid work requirement enacted in 2025, which takes effect on January 1, 2027 \[5\], shall be repealed. Medicaid eligibility shall be verified automatically from existing records, and enrollees shall keep continuous coverage until Universal Health Insurance covers them. A work requirement is incompatible with a universal coverage guarantee.

#### 4.7.2 Moving Enrollees into Universal Health Insurance

Medicaid enrollees shall move into Universal Health Insurance as its phases reach them (Section 5.1.6). No one shall lose coverage during the transition.

#### 4.7.3 Universal Health Insurance Partnership Compact

Each state may choose to join a Universal Health Insurance Partnership Compact.

- **A joining state** shall pay the Universal Health Insurance Trust Fund a fixed annual contribution equal to 90 percent of its average Medicaid spending in the three years before the compact, indexed for inflation and population. In return, Universal Health Insurance shall take over all of the state's Medicaid costs, including long-term care, and the federal government shall bear all future cost growth.
- **A state that does not join** shall keep its existing Medicaid funding and federal matching rate. Its residents shall still receive Universal Health Insurance. The state shall remain responsible for Medicaid services Universal Health Insurance does not yet provide, including long-term care.
- **Use of contributions:** compact contributions shall go only to the Universal Health Insurance Trust Fund (Section 27.3).
- **Clear terms:** the terms shall be fixed in statute, so each state knows exactly what it is agreeing to before it joins.

#### 4.7.4 Why the Compact Is Voluntary

In *NFIB v. Sebelius* (2012) the Supreme Court held that Congress could not coerce states into the Affordable Care Act's Medicaid expansion by threatening all of their existing Medicaid funding \[6\]. It let states decline the expansion without losing existing funds. Congress may offer new funds with new conditions, but it may not threaten existing funds to force states into a new program.

The closest precedent is the 2003 Medicare drug law, under which states make monthly payments to Medicare for drug costs it took over from Medicaid \[7\]. States challenged those payments in 2006, but the Supreme Court declined to hear the case, and it has never ruled on the merits. That law also predates *NFIB*, and it collects the payments by offsetting each state's Medicaid grant, a tool that will not exist once Universal Health Insurance replaces Medicaid.

The compact therefore requires no state to pay, threatens no state's existing funds, and does not order states to spend their own money on a federal program. It offers a better deal (a 10 percent saving, relief from long-term care costs, and protection from future cost growth) in exchange for a predictable contribution.

### 4.9 Honest Limits

- **No measure in this chapter has been scored.** The Child Tax Credit is the largest cost and comes last for that reason.
- **The work-incentive debate is real.** Simulations of the 2021 credit made permanent ranged from roughly 300,000 to 1.5 million parents leaving work, and studies of the actual 2021 payments found small or no effects \[8\]. This chapter pairs full refundability with an expanded work credit and keeps the credit's phase-out, but critics will continue to raise the higher estimates.
- **Eligibility rules will draw criticism from both sides.** Requiring lawful presence for the claiming parent will be attacked by immigrant advocates as too strict and by opponents as too loose, and verifying lawful presence adds administrative work.
- **Self-attestation creates some risk of error.** The 90-day grace period could let some ineligible households receive benefits for a few months. Checking existing records first, and rewarding states for accuracy, are meant to keep that small.
- **The 50 percent phase-out limit costs money.** Slower phase-outs extend benefits to more households further up the income scale. The limit may need adjustment once it is scored.
- **Unemployment insurance reform raises employers' costs.** A higher federal wage base is a tax increase on employers, felt most by small businesses, and states will resist federal standards.
- **States may decline the compact.** If many do, the Universal Health Insurance Trust Fund collects less than planned. Any gap shall be closed only within the Trust Fund or by slower phase-in, never from another revenue stream (Section 2.8). The long-term care relief that makes the compact attractive depends on a long-term care benefit that Section 5.1.7 lists as an open question.
- **The legal design is untested.** The compact is built to avoid the coercion problem identified in *NFIB*, but no court has ruled on an arrangement like it.
- **Current law is still being implemented.** The 2025 SNAP and Medicaid changes are taking effect in stages through 2030, so the starting point for these reforms will keep shifting.
