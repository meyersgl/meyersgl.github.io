## Chapter 2: Federal Tax Fairness

*Funding role: Chapter 27 assigns the Extreme Wealth Fair-Share Tax (Sections 2.1 to 2.3) to the Sovereign Wealth Reserve, with a smoothed transfer to the General Fund for tiers 3 and 4 only once the Reserve is full (Section 27.5). The federal tax fairness measures in Sections 2.6 to 2.10, with net revenue from the enforcement division in Section 2.4, are the platform's stable revenue for recurring programs (decisions 0004, 0008, 0022, 0034).*

A fair tax system asks the most from those who have gained the most, taxes income from wealth on the same terms as income from work, and does not let the largest fortunes pass between generations untaxed. It should also be honest about how high the combined rates become, and it should set limits on them. This chapter does both.

### 2.1 Extreme Wealth Fair-Share Tax \[Statute – High Resistance\]

An annual tax shall apply to the net worth of households and trusts above \$50 million, indexed for inflation, following the design of the Ultra-Millionaire Tax Act \[5\]:

- **Rates:** 2 percent of net worth above \$50 million, and 3 percent of net worth above \$1 billion. Only the portion above each threshold is taxed at the higher rate, so there is no cliff.
- **Base:** all assets worldwide, net of debts, with no large exemptions. The \$50 million threshold already leaves out ordinary homes, retirement savings, and family businesses; about 260,000 households, roughly the top 0.15 percent, would pay in the first year \[5\].
- **Valuation:** traded assets at market value; other assets under Section 2.3.
- **Exit tax:** a citizen or long-term resident with net worth above \$50 million who gives up citizenship or residence shall pay 40 percent of net worth above \$50 million.
- **Enforcement:** a minimum annual audit rate for covered households, third-party reporting of asset holdings, and the division in Section 2.4.
- **Revenue use:** Section 27.5. The tax first fills the Sovereign Wealth Reserve; only then does a smoothed share reach the General Fund, and only for tiers 3 and 4 of Section 27.9.
- **Severability:** if a court holds this tax invalid, the rest of this chapter stands, and programs paid from the smoothed transfer pause under Section 27.9 until replacement revenue is enacted. Tiers 1 and 2 never depend on this tax.

#### 2.1.1 Enhanced Anti-Evasion Framework \[Statute\]

Mandatory beneficial ownership reporting, asset aggregation requirements, global disclosure standards, concealment penalties, and strengthened exit-tax provisions shall prevent avoidance schemes. Aggregation rules shall cover trusts, family entities, and minor children.

### 2.2 Liquidity Smoothing Mechanism \[Statute\]

Covered taxpayers may satisfy certain obligations through transfers of non-voting equity. Equity received shall be held in a separate passive account, shall carry no government voting or control rights, shall not count toward the Sovereign Wealth Reserve's recession-stabilization balance, and shall be sold down on a defined schedule.

### 2.3 Valuation of Illiquid Assets \[Statute\]

Assets without a market price, such as private businesses, real estate, and art, shall be valued by the IRS using published formulas and, where a later sale shows the earlier value was too low, retrospective correction with interest. Discounts for lack of control or marketability shall not apply to passive assets held through a family entity (as in Section 2.10). A household whose covered wealth is mostly illiquid may defer the tax with interest until the asset is sold or transferred, or pay in non-voting equity under Section 2.2.

### 2.4 Targeted IRS Audits \[Statute\]

A dedicated High-Net-Worth and Complex-Entity Enforcement Division shall be statutorily ring-fenced. Its funding shall be restricted by appropriations rider to audits of filers above the Extreme Wealth Fair-Share Tax threshold and of large or complex corporate and partnership structures and shall not be used to audit ordinary filers. The IRS shall publicly report audit rates by income and wealth bracket, and the division shall be reauthorized every five years based on performance data. The division shall receive \$80 billion of mandatory funding over its first ten years, provided outside annual appropriations. Appendix E counts its net revenue at the Congressional Budget Office's estimated return, not at higher published estimates.

### 2.5 State Tax Progressivity Incentive \[Statute\]

Federal incentives shall encourage states to move toward more progressive taxation and away from highly regressive sales tax dependence.

### 2.6 Restored Top Income-Tax Rate \[Statute\]

The top federal income-tax rate on ordinary income shall be 39.6 percent, the rate in effect before 2018. It shall apply above the income level at which the 37 percent bracket begins under current law: $640,600 for single filers and $768,700 for married couples filing jointly in 2026, indexed for inflation. No other bracket shall change.

The 2025 reconciliation law made the 37 percent rate permanent, so this section is a tax increase relative to current law. It is a modest and familiar one: the same top rate applied for most of the 1990s and again from 2013 through 2017.

#### 2.6.1 Pass-Through Deduction Above the Top Bracket

The qualified business income deduction (Internal Revenue Code Section 199A) shall phase out for taxpayers whose taxable income exceeds the threshold in Section 2.6, falling to zero over the next \$100,000 of taxable income (\$200,000 for married couples filing jointly). Below the threshold the deduction is unchanged. Owners of pass-through businesses with top-bracket incomes then pay the same income-tax rate on business income as on wages.

### 2.7 Capital Income Parity \[Statute – High Resistance\]

Income from wealth should not be taxed more lightly than income from work at the top of the income scale. Today a household earning $5 million from wages pays 37 percent plus Medicare taxes on its last dollar, while a household earning $5 million from selling stock pays 20 percent plus the 3.8 percent net investment income tax. And gains held until death are never taxed at all.

#### 2.7.1 Gains and Dividends Taxed as Ordinary Income Above $1 Million

Long-term capital gains and qualified dividends shall be taxed at ordinary income rates to the extent a taxpayer's adjusted gross income exceeds $1 million, indexed for inflation. Only the portion above the threshold shall be taxed at the higher rate, so there is no cliff at $1 million. Below the threshold, current capital-gains rates shall continue to apply. The 3.8 percent net investment income tax shall remain, for a top rate on capital income of 43.4 percent before the Social Security contribution in Section 2.7.5.

#### 2.7.2 Carried Interest

Carried interest, the share of an investment fund's profits paid to the fund's managers for managing it, shall be taxed as ordinary income at every income level. It is pay for services, not a return on the managers' own capital.

#### 2.7.3 Transfers at Death and by Gift as Realization Events

Appreciated property transferred at death or by gift shall be treated as sold at its fair market value, and the gain shall be taxed as income to the person making the transfer, under these rules:

- **Exclusion:** the first $5 million of gains per person shall be exempt ($10 million per married couple, with any unused amount passing to the surviving spouse), indexed for inflation.
- **Existing protections kept:** the current exclusion for gains on a principal residence shall continue in addition to the $5 million exclusion. Transfers to a spouse or to charity shall not trigger tax; a spouse shall take over the original cost basis.
- **Family farms and businesses:** tax on a family-owned farm or business shall be deferred for as long as the family continues to own and operate it, and shall come due only when it is sold or stops being family-owned and operated. This keeps faith with Chapter 10's protection of family farms.
- **Illiquid assets:** tax on other illiquid assets may be paid over fifteen years, with interest.
- **Retirement accounts and personal belongings:** retirement accounts, which are already taxed when money is withdrawn, and ordinary personal belongings shall be excluded.

This section is what makes Section 2.7.1 work. Without it, the most effective way to avoid a higher capital-gains rate is to hold assets until death, when the gain disappears. The Penn Wharton Budget Model estimates that a 33 percent rate on capital gains raises the most revenue while gains escape tax at death, and that the figure rises to 42 percent once death is treated as a realization event \[3\]. The 43.4 percent top rate in Section 2.7.1 therefore depends on this section. The contribution in Section 2.7.5 raises the top rate on gains above that estimate (Section 2.13).

#### 2.7.4 No Gain Taxed Twice

The taxes in this chapter shall be coordinated so that no dollar of gain is taxed twice:

- Income tax owed at death under Section 2.7.3 shall be deductible in computing the estate tax under Section 2.10.
- The tax in Section 2.1 is a tax on net worth, not on income. It does not change an asset's tax basis and is not credited against income tax.

A family with more than \$50 million in wealth can be subject to the wealth tax, income tax on its gains, and the estate tax. Each reaches a different base: the wealth tax reaches the stock of wealth each year, the income taxes reach income as it is realized or transferred, and the estate tax reaches transfers. This section ensures the income and estate taxes never reach the same dollar.

#### 2.7.5 Social Security Contribution on Investment Income Above \$1 Million

A Social Security contribution of 12.4 percent shall apply to net investment income, as defined for the net investment income tax, to the extent a taxpayer's modified adjusted gross income exceeds \$1 million (\$2 million for married couples filing jointly), indexed for inflation. Net investment income includes gains, dividends, interest, rents, royalties, and passive business income. The rate matches the combined Social Security rate on wages, so a millionaire's investment income contributes on the same terms as a worker's pay. Wages and self-employment income are not subject to this contribution, so the 60 percent ceiling in Section 2.8 is unaffected. Revenue goes to the Social Security trust funds (Section 26.2.4) and earns no added benefits. With this contribution, the top federal rate on long-term gains and dividends is 55.8 percent, the same as the top rate on wages before the Universal Health Insurance contribution.

### 2.8 Combined Federal Wage Rate Ceiling \[Statute\]

The combined federal marginal tax rate on wages and on self-employment income shall not exceed 60 percent. The combined rate shall count:

- the top income-tax rate (Section 2.6);
- both the employee and employer shares of Social Security and Medicare payroll taxes, including the additional 0.9 percent Medicare tax, because economists generally find that workers bear the employer share through lower pay;
- every Universal Health Insurance contribution that applies to wages, whether a payroll, employer, or income-based contribution;
- the Paid Leave Trust Fund contribution (Section 3.3), which applies only to wages up to its wage base.

Under the rates in this chapter and in Chapter 26, the income-tax and payroll-tax components come to 55.8 percent at the top: 39.6 percent income tax, 12.4 percent Social Security once the cap is eliminated, and 3.8 percent Medicare. The remaining room under the ceiling is 4.2 percentage points.

**How the ceiling works.** The Universal Health Insurance contribution on wages above the top-bracket threshold shall be set no higher than the room remaining under the ceiling, 4.2 points under current rates. If implementing legislation for Section 26.2.1 applies less than the full employer share to wages above the taxable maximum, the room grows by the same amount, but the ceiling stays at 60 percent. No other tax in this platform may be raised on wages if doing so would breach the ceiling.

**Protecting the Universal Health Insurance Trust Fund.** Any revenue the ceiling keeps the Trust Fund from collecting shall be made up only from the Trust Fund's own sources in Section 27.3: the broad-based contribution rates and the savings from consolidated health financing. If those are not enough, Universal Health Insurance shall phase in more slowly under Section 27.9. The General Fund, the Extreme Wealth Fair-Share Tax, and carbon revenue shall never cover the gap.

**What this edition can and cannot show.** The ceiling limits only the marginal rate on wages above roughly $640,000 a year. It leaves untouched the contribution rates that apply to nearly all of the wage base, which are where nearly all Trust Fund revenue comes from. That is why the ceiling's effect on the Trust Fund should be small. But Universal Health Insurance contribution rates have not yet been set or scored (Section 5.1.2), and this edition contains no dollar estimates (Section 27.12). This edition therefore does not claim that the Trust Fund balances with the ceiling in place. It commits instead to the rule above: any shortfall is closed inside the Trust Fund or by slower phase-in, never by borrowing from another stream. Independent scoring of the contribution schedule shall report the ceiling's revenue effect separately.

State income taxes are not counted in the federal ceiling. In the highest-tax states the combined federal and state rate on top wages can exceed 70 percent. The ceiling caps the federal share; it cannot cap state choices.

### 2.9 Strengthened Corporate Minimum Tax \[Statute\]

The corporate alternative minimum tax on the financial-statement income of corporations averaging more than $1 billion a year shall rise from 15 percent to 21 percent, matching the regular corporate rate. The regular corporate income-tax rate shall remain 21 percent.

- **Protected incentives:** the research credit and credits for domestic advanced manufacturing and clean-energy production shall continue to be allowed in full against the minimum tax, so the minimum tax does not undo the platform's support for research and domestic production (Chapters 11 and 18).
- **Disclosure:** each covered corporation shall publicly report the difference between the profits it reports to shareholders and the profits it reports to the IRS, and the main reasons for that difference.

The purpose is simple: a large, profitable company should pay at least the regular corporate rate on the profits it reports to its own shareholders.

### 2.10 Estate and Gift Tax Reform \[Statute – High Resistance\]

The estate, gift, and generation-skipping transfer tax exemption shall be $5 million per person, indexed for inflation from enactment, with any unused exemption passing to a surviving spouse as under current law. The top rate shall remain 40 percent.

The main avoidance tools shall be closed:

- **Short-term grantor trusts:** grantor retained annuity trusts shall have a minimum term of ten years and a minimum taxable gift at creation.
- **Valuation discounts:** discounts for lack of control or marketability shall not apply to passive assets such as cash and marketable securities held through a family partnership or company.
- **Dynasty trusts:** the generation-skipping exemption shall shelter a trust for no more than 50 years.

Existing relief for family farms and businesses, including special-use valuation and payment over time, shall continue. Income tax paid at death under Section 2.7.3 shall be deductible (Section 2.7.4).

The Extreme Wealth Fair-Share Tax already reaches fortunes above $50 million each year. The estate tax's job in this platform is the range between $5 million and $50 million, where closing avoidance tools matters more than raising the rate.

### 2.11 Proposed Rates, Scoring, and Revenue Use \[Statute\]

The rates and thresholds in Sections 2.6 to 2.10 are proposed rates, published now so the public can see what the platform asks and so the combined-rate disclosure in Section 27.11 can be calculated. They shall be scored by the Joint Committee on Taxation and the Congressional Budget Office before enactment.

- **Revenue use:** all revenue from Sections 2.6 to 2.10, except the Social Security contribution in Section 2.7.5, and net revenue from the enforcement division in Section 2.4, shall go to the General Fund for the recurring programs listed in Section 27.4, and to nothing else (Section 27.3). The contribution in Section 2.7.5 goes to the Social Security trust funds. Collections of the tax in Section 2.1 follow Section 27.5.
- **If scoring falls short:** if scored revenue is lower than the programs it supports require, those programs shall phase in more slowly under Sections 27.6 and 27.9. The rates in this chapter shall not rise automatically, and the ceiling in Section 2.8 shall continue to apply.
- **Review:** the Congressional Budget Office shall report every five years on revenue collected under each section compared with its score.

### 2.13 Honest Limits

- **Revenue is not yet scored.** Comparable published estimates, taken from different models and baselines and not additive, put the stable General Fund measures in this chapter (Sections 2.4 and 2.6 to 2.10, other than Section 2.7.5) in an illustrative range of about \$1.2 trillion to \$1.8 trillion over ten years (Appendix E). The best match is for Section 2.6: a 2026 Congressional Research Service analysis of the same rate and threshold found about \$459 billion to \$465 billion \[4\]. Estimates for taxing gains at ordinary rates and at death, about \$183 billion to \$289 billion, predate the 2025 law \[1\], and the estimate for the corporate minimum tax is an upper bound. None of these is this platform's score. Capital-gains revenue is especially uncertain, because people can time their sales.
- **Wealth-tax estimates differ threefold.** For the same design, the Penn Wharton Budget Model estimated about \$2.1 trillion over 2022 to 2031 \[6\], while Emmanuel Saez and Gabriel Zucman estimate about \$6.2 trillion over 2026 to 2035, after top fortunes roughly doubled \[5\]. European wealth taxes raised far less, largely because of exemptions and avoidance. The smoothed transfer in Section 27.5 exists because this revenue cannot be predicted.
- **The constitutional question is open, and the wealth tax carries the most risk.** An annual tax on net worth may be challenged as a direct tax that the Constitution requires to be apportioned among the states by population. In *Moore v. United States* (2024) the Supreme Court also declined to decide whether the Sixteenth Amendment requires income to be realized before it can be taxed \[2\]. Section 2.7.3 is the safest design available, because a transfer at death or by gift is a clear event that the law can treat as a sale, but it is not risk-free. The severability rule in Section 2.1 keeps tiers 1 and 2 safe if the wealth tax falls.
- **The top rate on gains may pass the revenue-maximizing level.** With the Social Security contribution in Section 2.7.5, the top rate on long-term gains is 55.8 percent. The Penn Wharton Budget Model estimates that revenue from gains peaks at about 42 percent even when gains are taxed at death \[3\]. Above that, people hold assets longer and the contribution may raise little or nothing from gains, though it still reaches dividends, interest, and rents. Its estimate in Appendix E is set low for this reason.
- **The combined rates are high.** A 60 percent federal ceiling on wages is well above today's top federal rate of 40.8 percent, and state taxes come on top. Higher rates give high earners more reason to relabel wages as capital income or corporate income, which is why Sections 2.6.1, 2.7, and 26.3 tax those forms on similar terms. For the wealthiest families, the wealth tax and the taxes on gains together can exceed their annual return in a weak market year; opponents will call this confiscation. Behavioral responses will reduce revenue below a simple calculation.
- **Large estates face three taxes.** Section 2.7.4 prevents the income and estate taxes from reaching the same dollar, but a very large estate will still pay more than under any recent law, and opponents will call it a triple death tax. The platform's answer is that each tax reaches income or wealth that has otherwise escaped tax.
- **The corporate minimum tax is complex.** The current 15 percent tax has required repeated interim guidance from the IRS \[10\]. Raising its rate raises the stakes of getting the rules right.
- **Enforcement funding can be cut.** Mandatory funding for the division in Section 2.4 makes cuts harder, not impossible: a later Congress can rescind it, as Congress did with most of the 2022 enforcement funding.
