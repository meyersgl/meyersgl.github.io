## Chapter 3: Work, Wages & Family

*Funding role: the living wage, labor rights, and licensing reforms change rules and cost little. Paid leave is paid by the Paid Leave Trust Fund from its own payroll contribution, never the General Fund (Section 3.3). Childcare cost caps are paid from the General Fund in tier 2. Eldercare is health care: long-term care is a Universal Health Insurance benefit paid by its Trust Fund (Section 5.1.1). Universal Adjustment Assistance training and triggered apprenticeships are paid from the General Fund within one capped amount in tier 1, and wage insurance from the unemployment trust funds (Sections 3.6.7 to 3.6.9). Implementation order is governed by Section 27.9.*

### 3.1 Living Wage Framework \[Statute\]

The federal minimum wage shall be set as a fixed percentage of the federal poverty line and adjusted for regional cost of living using federal regional price data. To avoid sharp differences across metropolitan boundaries, the regional adjustment shall phase in gradually across a buffer zone (a linear taper over a defined distance) rather than changing at the boundary line.

### 3.2 Protecting the Right to Unionize \[Statute\]

Workers shall have strong protections against retaliation and unfair labor practices, including faster union election timelines, limits on mandatory anti-union meetings, and meaningful penalties for illegal retaliation. Bargaining shall remain decentralized and voluntary at the firm level.

### 3.3 Paid Leave Framework \[Statute\]

All workers shall have access to paid family, parental, caregiving, and personal medical leave regardless of employer size, for up to 12 weeks per year. The weekly benefit shall replace 90 percent of a worker's wages up to half the national average weekly wage and 50 percent of wages above that, up to a maximum of about \$1,500 a week, indexed to wage growth, so that lower-paid workers can afford to take leave. Wage replacement shall be paid from a Paid Leave Trust Fund, a social-insurance pool financed by a dedicated payroll contribution shared equally by employers and employees, so that no single employer bears the full cost of a worker's leave. The contribution shall apply to wages up to the Social Security taxable maximum in effect before Section 26.2.1, indexed to wage growth, and shall be set by independent scoring at the rate needed to keep the Trust Fund solvent. The rate may adjust each year within a band fixed in statute, on certification by the Trust Fund's actuaries, to maintain solvency; any change outside the band requires legislation. It counts toward the combined-rate ceiling in Section 2.8. Because it does not apply to wages in the top bracket, it does not reduce the room left under that ceiling for the Universal Health Insurance contribution. The Trust Fund shall never draw on the General Fund. Job-protection requirements may be phased in for the smallest employers, but wage replacement shall be available to all workers.

### 3.4 Care Economy Infrastructure \[Statute – High Resistance\]

Childcare from birth to school entry shall have costs capped on a sliding scale relative to household income, with the cap phasing in smoothly rather than ending at an income cliff.

Public funds shall support provider pay, workforce training, and facility capacity so that affordable care is actually available, particularly for infants and in rural areas.

Eldercare is health care. Long-term care for older adults, including home care, is a benefit of Universal Health Insurance under Section 5.1.1, paid by its Trust Fund, with room-and-board costs capped on a sliding scale and support for family caregivers.

**Honest limit:** Capping what families pay raises demand for care faster than the supply of care workers can grow. Without enough providers, caps can lengthen waiting lists rather than lower costs. Funding for provider pay and training is meant to close that gap, but the care workforce is low-paid and slow to expand. The childcare caps are paid from the General Fund in tier 2 and wait behind earlier tiers (Section 27.9). Eldercare depends on the Universal Health Insurance long-term care benefit, whose design and cost remain an open question (Section 5.1.7).

### 3.5 Workforce Mobility & Occupational Licensing Reform \[Statute\]

Occupational licensing shall exist only where justified by public safety.

#### 3.5.1 Interstate License Recognition

Workers licensed in one state should generally be able to practice in another without repeating equivalent qualification requirements.

#### 3.5.2 Apprenticeship Expansion

Earn-while-you-learn career pathways shall be expanded through federal partnerships. Apprenticeships in AI-exposed occupations expand automatically under the trigger in Section 3.6.8, within the funding cap in Section 3.6.9.

#### 3.5.3 Military Credential Recognition

Military certifications and experience shall receive expedited civilian recognition.

### 3.6 Technology and AI at Work \[Statute – High Resistance\]

*Funding role: most of this section creates rights and duties and costs little. Wage insurance is paid from unemployment trust funds. Training grants under Universal Adjustment Assistance, and any apprenticeship expansion triggered under Section 3.6.8, share one capped General Fund amount in tier 1, step 5 (Section 27.9) (decision 0025).*

Automated tools now help decide who is hired, how work is scheduled and measured, and who is disciplined or let go. Used well, they can make work safer and more productive. Used carelessly, they can make consequential decisions no person has reviewed, monitor workers in ways no supervisor could, and quietly remove the entry-level jobs where careers begin. The evidence so far shows no economy-wide displacement. But employment of workers ages 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations is about 19 percent below where it would be had it kept pace with less-exposed peers, and the declines are concentrated where AI replaces tasks rather than assisting workers \[1\]. This section gives workers notice, explanation, a human decision-maker, limits on surveillance, a voice in workplace change, and support when jobs disappear. It does not tax or slow the technology itself.

#### 3.6.1 Scope

This section applies to any automated decision system an employer uses to make, or substantially influence, decisions about:

- hiring;
- pay;
- scheduling;
- assignment;
- promotion;
- productivity standards;
- discipline;
- firing;
- the deactivation of workers on digital platforms.

An automated decision system is any machine-learning, statistical, or AI-based process that scores, ranks, classifies, recommends, or decides. Ordinary tools such as spam filters, spreadsheets, and databases are excluded. Workers on digital platforms are covered whether they are classified as employees or as independent contractors.

#### 3.6.2 A Human Decision-Maker

- **Consequential decisions:** no worker shall be fired, disciplined, or deactivated on the basis of an automated decision system's output alone. A person with authority to change the outcome shall review the relevant information independently before the decision takes effect.
- **Applicants:** a job applicant rejected with the substantial help of an automated decision system may request an explanation and a review by a person.

These requirements carry out the human-review right in Section 13.5.2.

#### 3.6.3 Notice, Explanation, and Data Access

- **Before use:** employers shall tell workers and applicants, before the system is used, which decisions involve an automated decision system and what kinds of data it uses.
- **After a decision:** a worker or applicant affected by a consequential decision may obtain a plain-language explanation of the main factors behind it.
- **Data access:** workers may see the data about them that such systems use, and have errors corrected.

#### 3.6.4 Bias Audits and Shared Responsibility

- **Vendor audits:** developers of automated decision systems sold for hiring or promotion shall obtain an independent bias audit each year, testing for disparate impact on the protected traits in Section 13.3.1. They shall publish a summary and give the full audit to their customers.
- **Employers:** an employer may rely on its vendor's current audit, instead of commissioning its own.
- **Shared responsibility:** a vendor whose system makes or substantially influences an employment decision on an employer's behalf shall share responsibility with the employer for unlawful discrimination resulting from the system's design.

Placing the audit duty on the relatively few developers, not on every employer, keeps compliance costs low. This is the approach that avoids the burdens that led Colorado to repeal its broader law \[2\].

#### 3.6.5 Workplace Surveillance

**Prohibited practices.** Employers shall not:

- use tools that infer workers' emotions, mental states, or personality from their faces, voices, or bodies;
- collect neural data, or biometric data beyond what is needed to secure facilities and systems;
- monitor workers while they are off duty, or on their personal devices, except to secure employer data on a device the worker has chosen to use for work;
- use any monitoring in bathrooms, lactation rooms, or break areas;
- monitor or use automated systems to predict or interfere with union activity or other protected concerted activity.

**Accessibility exception.** The ban on neural and biometric data does not apply to assistive or accessibility technology that a worker with a disability has voluntarily requested as an accommodation (Sections 13.5.2 and 13.5.4). Data collected through that technology may be used only to provide the accommodation. It shall not be used for evaluation, discipline, or any other employment decision, and it shall be deleted when no longer needed for the accommodation.

**Notice for all other monitoring.** All other electronic monitoring requires advance notice of what is monitored, how, and why.

**Productivity quotas.** Productivity quotas set or enforced by automated systems shall be disclosed to the workers subject to them. No worker shall be penalized for time spent on breaks required by law, on safety compliance, or on bathroom use.

#### 3.6.6 Workers' Voice in Technological Change

- **Notice:** an employer with 50 or more employees shall give affected workers 90 days' notice before introducing technology that will substantially change their job duties, eliminate their positions, or change how their performance is measured.
- **Bargaining:** where workers are represented by a union, the introduction of such technology and its effects shall be a mandatory subject of bargaining.

This is consistent with the decentralized, firm-level bargaining in Section 3.2, and it is written into statute so that it does not depend on the views of a National Labor Relations Board whose members can now be removed at will.

#### 3.6.7 Universal Adjustment Assistance

Trade Adjustment Assistance, which helped workers who lost jobs to imports, expired in June 2022 \[3\]. It shall be replaced by Universal Adjustment Assistance for any worker displaced by trade, technological change, or industrial restructuring. Workers need not prove the specific cause of the job loss.

- **Wage insurance:** a reemployed worker whose new job pays less than the old one shall receive half the difference for up to two years, up to a statutory annual limit. Wage insurance shall be paid from unemployment trust funds through a small addition to unemployment contributions, administered with the unemployment insurance system in Section 4.4.
- **Training:** eligible workers may receive training grants for programs with a record of placing graduates in jobs, including apprenticeships under Section 3.5.2. Training grants are paid from the General Fund under Section 3.6.9.
- **Layoff notice:** the Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification Act shall require 90 days' notice of mass layoffs and plant closings, up from 60, including layoffs resulting from automation.

Universal Adjustment Assistance is separate from the carbon Transition Fund (Section 20.2.2), and neither shall be counted toward the other.

#### 3.6.8 Protecting the Entry-Level Career Ladder

The Bureau of Labor Statistics shall publish quarterly indicators of employment, hiring, and wages by occupation's exposure to AI and by age, with an annual report to Congress.

If employment of workers ages 22 to 25 in the most-exposed occupations falls more than a threshold set in statute below that of comparable less-exposed workers for four consecutive quarters, apprenticeship funding under Section 3.5.2 shall expand automatically for those occupations. The expansion shall continue until the gap narrows below the threshold.

#### 3.6.9 Funding Guardrails

- **Shared cap:** training grants under Section 3.6.7 and any apprenticeship expansion triggered under Section 3.6.8 shall share a single annual General Fund amount, capped in statute at \$5 billion a year, indexed for inflation (about \$55 billion over ten years).
- **Phase-in order:** the capped amount is placed in tier 1, step 5 of the General Fund priority order, after both parts of the Child Tax Credit (Section 27.9). It shall take effect only when its independently scored cost is covered by enacted federal tax fairness revenue (Sections 27.6 and 27.10).
- **When the trigger fires:** a triggered apprenticeship expansion shall be paid within the cap. If the cap is insufficient, the expansion shall be phased in over several years rather than raising the cap automatically. Any increase in the cap requires new legislation with its own scored revenue (Section 27.6).
- **Other costs:** wage insurance is paid only from unemployment trust funds. Enforcement by the Department of Labor and measurement by the Bureau of Labor Statistics are core government functions within capped and scored base appropriations (as in Section 13.8).

#### 3.6.10 No Tax on Automation

The platform does not tax automation itself. A tax on technology that replaces workers would be hard to define, would encourage moving work and investment abroad, and would work against the platform's commitments to research and domestic production (Chapter 18). The gains from new technology are shared instead through the federal tax fairness measures in Chapter 2 and the protections in this section.

#### 3.6.11 Enforcement and State Laws

- **Who enforces:** workers and applicants may sue to enforce this section and recover reasonable attorney fees, and state attorneys general may enforce it (Section 13.7.1). The Department of Labor may also enforce it, with civil penalties scaled to employer size.
- **No forced arbitration:** pre-dispute arbitration agreements and class-action waivers shall not apply to claims under this section (Section 13.7.3).
- **State laws:** this section sets a minimum standard, consistent with Section 19.6. It does not preempt state or local laws that give workers greater protection.
- **Small employers:** employers with fewer than 15 employees shall have an additional year to comply with Sections 3.6.2 through 3.6.5.

#### 3.6.12 Honest Limits

- **The evidence is early.** The strongest evidence so far comes from payroll data covering mid-2026. It shows effects concentrated on young workers in exposed occupations, not economy-wide displacement \[1\]. The trigger in Section 3.6.8 is designed so that money is spent only if those effects continue, but the right threshold is a judgment call.
- **Human review can become a rubber stamp.** A reviewer who approves every automated recommendation satisfies the letter of Section 3.6.2 but not its purpose. The duty to review the relevant information independently, together with the right to an explanation, is meant to prevent that, but it will be hard to enforce.
- **Bias audits have limits.** Audit methods are still developing, and an audit can miss problems that appear only in a particular workplace. Shared vendor responsibility will be strongly opposed by software companies.
- **Federal and state conflict is likely.** Executive Order 14365 directs the Justice Department to challenge state AI laws \[4\]. This section protects stronger state laws by statute, but until it is enacted, state protections remain under legal and financial pressure. Businesses will argue that varying state rules raise costs, and the answer is that the federal minimum already covers the core protections.
- **Surveillance limits will be contested.** Logistics and warehouse employers will oppose the quota rules, and the line between permitted security monitoring and prohibited surveillance will need to be drawn in regulations and cases. The accessibility exception must be policed so that it is not used as a back door for collecting biometric data.
- **Wage insurance raises unemployment contributions.** Paying for wage insurance through the unemployment system keeps it off the General Fund, but it is a small payroll cost borne in practice by workers and employers.
- **The training budget is capped.** Universal Adjustment Assistance is broader than the program it replaces, but its training grants come from a capped amount behind three other tier-1 priorities. In a fast-moving period of displacement, the cap could bind before need is met, and raising it would require new revenue.
- **Not taxing automation will be criticized.** Some will argue that automation gains should be taxed directly. The platform's position is that Chapter 2 captures those gains where they show up, in high incomes and capital gains, without penalizing investment.
