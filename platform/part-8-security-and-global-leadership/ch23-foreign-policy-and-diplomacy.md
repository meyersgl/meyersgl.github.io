## Chapter 23: Foreign Policy & Diplomacy

### 23.1 Guiding Principle

Lasting security is built not only through military strength, but through diplomacy, partnership, economic cooperation, and mutual trust.

The United States should seek a world in which more nations view America as a trusted partner than as an adversary.

Military power remains an essential instrument of national defense, but diplomacy should remain the first instrument of foreign policy whenever practical.

### 23.2 Defensive Foreign Policy \[Statute / Executive\]

The United States shall maintain military capabilities sufficient to:

- Defend the nation

- Protect allies

- Secure global trade

- Deter aggression

The nation shall end foreign regime-change operations and shall reduce reliance on:

- Long-term occupations

- Open-ended military engagements

whenever practical.

**Forward Presence & Burden-Sharing**

No new permanent overseas military base shall be established without congressional authorization. Allies shall share defense burdens through periodic burden-sharing reviews, and the Department of Defense shall report to Congress at regular intervals on global force posture, including where maritime and over-the-horizon capabilities can substitute for forward presence. Ending regime-change operations is a core commitment that does not depend on force-posture decisions.

**Peace Through Strength**

Military strength exists primarily to prevent war, not seek it.

Cross-reference: See Chapter 24 Defense.

### 23.3 Diplomacy First Framework \[Statute\]

The United States shall prioritize:

- Diplomacy

- Conflict prevention

- Alliance management

- Economic cooperation

- Peaceful dispute resolution

before the use of military force.

**Diplomacy First Principle**

Military action should remain available when necessary, but policymakers should exhaust reasonable diplomatic alternatives before resorting to armed conflict.

### 23.4 Alliance & Strategic Partnership Initiative \[Statute\]

The United States shall maintain and strengthen alliances with democratic partners around the world.

Priority objectives include:

- Defense cooperation

- Economic cooperation

- Scientific collaboration

- Intelligence sharing

- Joint security planning

Strong alliances reduce the likelihood of conflict and multiply collective security.

Cross-reference: See Chapter 24 Defense.

### 23.5 Strategic Foreign Assistance Program \[Statute\]

Foreign assistance should advance both humanitarian values and long-term stability.

Federal foreign-aid programs shall support three primary objectives:

#### 23.5.1 Humanitarian Stability

Programs may support:

- Disaster relief

- Food security

- Refugee assistance

- Clean water access

- Public health initiatives

The objective is reducing human suffering and preventing instability before it becomes crisis.

#### 23.5.2 Economic Development

Programs may support:

- Education

- Workforce development

- Agricultural modernization

- Infrastructure investment

- Disease prevention

Economic development reduces long-term instability and creates future trading partners.

#### 23.5.3 Democratic Resilience

The United States may support initiatives that encourage:

- Transparent governance

- Anti-corruption reforms

- Civil society development

- Independent institutions

- Rule of law

The objective is supporting stable societies rather than imposing political outcomes.

#### 23.5.4 Reproductive Health in Foreign Assistance \[Statute – High Resistance\]

Since 1973, the Helms Amendment has barred foreign assistance from paying for abortion "as a method of family planning," and it has been applied as a total ban, even after rape or incest or when a patient's life is at risk. Separately, the Mexico City Policy, which each administration since 1985 has rescinded or reinstated by executive action, bars foreign organizations that receive U.S. aid from providing or discussing abortion with their own funds. In January 2026 it was expanded through three final rules to most non-military foreign assistance and to new subjects beyond abortion, reaching as much as $39.8 billion in aid and almost 2,600 prime recipients \[1\]\[2\]. This section ends both policies by statute, on terms consistent with Chapter 6.

- **Helms repeal.** Section 104(f) of the Foreign Assistance Act of 1961 and the corresponding provision of the annual foreign operations appropriations act shall be repealed.
- **Host-country law.** Foreign assistance may fund abortion services only where the law of the host country permits them. Post-abortion care and emergency treatment remain eligible regardless of the legal status of abortion under host-country law.
- **No global gag rule.** The United States shall not condition eligibility for foreign assistance on an organization's lawful speech, advocacy, counseling, referral practices, or provision of health services conducted with non-United States funds where those activities are lawful under the laws of the host country. No future policy of the Mexico City type may be adopted by executive action. The prohibition follows the Appendix C approach of writing major rules into statute rather than leaving them to reversible executive discretion.
- **Anti-coercion rule (Kemp-Kasten).** The bar on funding any organization that supports or participates in coercive abortion or involuntary sterilization is kept. A decision to cut off an organization under it shall rest on published evidence, with written reasons and notice to Congress (Appendix C).
- **Lobbying rule (Siljander).** The bar on using aid funds to lobby for or against abortion is kept. It applies equally in both directions.
- **Protection from annual riders.** Restrictions on lawful reproductive health services funded through foreign assistance may be enacted only through amendment of the Foreign Assistance Act and not through annual appropriations riders. A Senate point of order shall enforce this rule.

**Interim executive steps.** Until the statute is enacted, the executive branch shall, on its first day:

- apply the Helms Amendment as written, so that it no longer bars funding for abortion after rape or incest, when, in reasonable medical judgment, the patient's life or physical or mental health is at risk (the standard in Section 6.2.1), or for post-abortion care, subject to the host-country law rule above;
- begin rescinding the three 2026 foreign assistance rules (Protecting Life in Foreign Assistance, Combating Gender Ideology in Foreign Assistance, and Combating Discriminatory Equity Ideology in Foreign Assistance). Because they were issued as final rules, their repeal requires notice-and-comment rulemaking and will take months.

Durability comes from the statute, not from executive action.

**Funding.** These provisions operate within existing foreign assistance appropriations and have no cost under Chapter 27.

**Honest limits.**

- **Helms has outlasted every Congress since 1973,** including those that supported abortion rights. Repeal faces more resistance than ending the Hyde Amendment, and will be described as spending tax dollars on abortion abroad.
- **The host-country rule limits reach.** In countries that ban abortion, U.S. aid will not fund it, even after rape. The platform accepts that limit to respect the law of the countries it works with.
- **A future Congress can still add a rider** by waiving the point of order.
- **The 2026 rules remain in force until rescinded,** and repeal through rulemaking will be challenged.

### 23.6 Accountability Standards for Foreign Assistance \[Statute\]

All major aid programs shall include:

- Performance metrics

- Independent audits

- Transparency reporting

- Outcome evaluations

Foreign assistance should be held to the same accountability standards expected of domestic programs.

Cross-reference: See Chapter 14 Government Effectiveness.

### 23.7 Friendship Through Partnership Principle

People who experience American assistance, cooperation, and goodwill are more likely to become future partners than future adversaries.

Economic cooperation, educational exchanges, scientific partnerships, and humanitarian assistance can strengthen international relationships while advancing American interests.

### 23.8 International Scientific & Educational Cooperation \[Statute\]

The United States shall encourage peaceful international collaboration involving:

- Scientific research

- Medical innovation

- Educational exchanges

- Space exploration

- Technology development

Such cooperation promotes both security and prosperity.

Cross-reference: See Chapter 18.
