# APPENDIX B: IMPLEMENTATION & LEGISLATIVE STRATEGY

## Purpose

A platform is only as good as its path into law. This appendix sets out how the platform would pass Congress: the Senate rules it would need, the legislative vehicle for each plank, the order of the first year, the limited role of executive action, and how the programs paid from the General Fund are phased in. It is written for the Congress and the Senate as they are, not as advocates might wish them to be.

## B.1 The Legislative Constraint

**The filibuster.** Most legislation needs 60 votes in the Senate to end debate. Calls in 2025 and 2026 to abolish that rule for legislation were not acted on; in September 2026 the Senate majority leader estimated that 25 to 30 senators of his own party favored keeping it \[1\]. The Senate has already used majority votes to end the 60-vote rule for executive and judicial nominations, so a majority can change the rule for legislation if it chooses.

**Budget reconciliation.** A reconciliation bill cannot be filibustered, but the Byrd rule limits what it may contain. Every provision must change spending or revenue, and a provision is struck if its budget effect is "merely incidental" to its policy purpose. Even very large budget effects can fail that test \[2\]. Reconciliation may not change Social Security, and may not increase the deficit in years after the budget window \[3\]. The parliamentarian has struck a minimum-wage increase and an immigration overhaul on these grounds \[4\].

**What this means for the platform.**

- Chapter 27's rule that every program arrives with its own scored revenue fits reconciliation well. The fiscal core of the platform can pass with 51 votes.
- Social Security reform (Chapter 26) can never pass through reconciliation.
- The democracy, civil rights, reproductive health, and accountability chapters have only a modest budget effect, so they need either 60 votes or the Senate rules reform in Section B.2.

## B.2 Senate Rules Reform \[Senate Rule\]

The Senate shall adopt a standing rule that replaces the 60-vote requirement with a process that lets majorities decide after real debate, while giving the minority a guaranteed voice.

1. **Fully paid-for bills.** A bill that the Congressional Budget Office and the Joint Committee on Taxation score as fully paid for under Section 27.10 may pass by majority vote after a guaranteed period of debate and the amendment votes in rule 3.
2. **All other legislation.** Debate may be ended by a declining threshold: 60 votes on the first cloture vote, then 57, then 54, then a simple majority, with several session days between votes.
3. **Minority rights.** The minority is guaranteed votes on a fixed number of its amendments and on a complete substitute amendment before any final vote.
4. **Repeal symmetry.** A bill that repeals or substantially cuts a program follows the same process as the bill that enacted it. A program enacted as a fully paid-for bill can be repealed by majority only under the same rules.
5. **Qualification.** Whether a bill is fully paid for is determined by CBO and Joint Committee on Taxation scoring under Section 27.10, not by the presiding officer or the majority leader.

**Why this design.** Abolishing the filibuster would let the platform pass, but would also let a later narrow majority repeal Universal Health Insurance in a week. The declining threshold guarantees delay and debate without a permanent veto. The paid-for rule turns Chapter 27's fiscal discipline into procedure: a majority can pass anything, if it pays for it.

**How it would be adopted.** Changing a standing rule normally takes two-thirds of the Senate. In practice, a majority would have to set a new precedent by majority vote, as both parties did for nominations in 2013 and 2017. Adopting it would require at least 50 senators and the Vice President.

## B.4 Sequencing the First Year

The first year runs three tracks at once, following the order in Section 27.9 (low-cost reforms first, then revenue, then programs):

1. **Reconciliation track.** A budget resolution with reconciliation instructions, followed by one bill that enacts the federal tax fairness revenue together with the Tier 1 programs it funds. Revenue and programs pass in the same bill, so no program is enacted without its funding.
2. **Democracy track.** A package combining automatic voter registration (Section 12.2), election integrity (Section 12.8), Voting Rights Act restoration (Section 13.2), and the ethics reforms in Chapter 15, introduced early and paired with the Senate rules reform in Section B.2. This forces the rules question at the start of the term rather than the end.
3. **Bipartisan track.** Permitting, research, infrastructure, AI safety, and national service bills, moved in parallel to show that the platform can govern with the other party where agreement exists.

Later reconciliation bills carry Tiers 2 and 3 as revenue is scored and enacted. Social Security reform proceeds through regular order once the rules reform is in place, or through a bipartisan commission if it is not.

## B.5 The First 100 Days

Executive action is limited. Orders that one president signs, the next can reverse, and the platform's own governance chapters warn against government by executive order. In the first 100 days the executive branch shall:

- **Reverse prior actions** that conflict with the platform, including Executive Order 14321 on homelessness and civil commitment (Section 8.3.5) and the Continuum of Care funding conditions that abandoned Housing First (Section 8.3.1).
- **Begin implementation:** start the rulemaking, staffing, and data systems each program will need, such as automatic SNAP verification (Section 4.5.2) and the rent-roll reporting system (Section 8.2.5), so that programs can take effect promptly once enacted.
- **Take narrow actions within existing authority** that the platform supports: begin the process of removing cannabis from the controlled-substances schedule and grant clemency for federal cannabis possession (Section 16.11); decline to renew for-profit detention contracts as they expire (Section 16.6); issue SNAP guidance that uses every exemption and waiver current law allows (Section 4.5); and resume pattern-or-practice investigations with published reasons for any case closed (Section 16.14).
- **Send Congress on day one** the budget resolution, the democracy package, and the Senate rules reform resolution.

## B.6 Phasing In General Fund Programs

Section 27.9 sets the order. In summary:

**Tiers.** Open-ended programs, whose cost grows with the number of people who qualify, are ranked:

1. Income security (Chapter 4), then training and apprenticeships (Sections 3.6.7 to 3.6.9), with the Disabled Access Credit (Section 13.5.1).
2. Housing vouchers for people experiencing or at imminent risk of homelessness; eviction prevention; universal preschool and childcare cost caps; eldercare cost caps.
3. Tuition-free public higher education; student-loan reform; K-12 equity funding.
4. Universal housing vouchers.

**Fixed-budget programs.** Research expansion, national service, the public housing developer's operations, state rent stabilization grants, and the enhanced benefits of the National Farm Risk Protection Program are not ranked in the tiers. They follow these rules:

- **R1.** Each continues at its current-law funding level.
- **R2.** Expansion above current-law levels occurs only after Tier 1 is fully funded.
- **R3.** Expansion remains subject to a fixed statutory ceiling for each program and to the overall spending cap (Section 27.6).
- **R4.** Construction of long-lived assets, such as public housing and research facilities, uses the Capital Investment Account (Section 27.5) and the borrowing rule in Section 27.7.
- **R5.** Paid leave is not a General Fund program. It is paid by a self-financing trust fund supported by a dedicated payroll contribution counted toward the combined-rate ceiling (Sections 2.8 and 3.3).
- **R6.** The National Farm Risk Protection Program preserves current-law crop insurance commitments, while its enhanced benefits remain subject to fixed statutory ceilings.

## B.7 Honest Limits

- **The rules reform needs 50 votes.** Senators of both parties have defended the 60-vote rule, and neither party has been willing to change it for legislation. Without Section B.2, the regular-order planks in Section B.3 depend on bipartisan agreement that may not exist.
- **The paid-for rule makes scoring political.** If passage depends on a CBO score, the scorekeepers and their methods will come under pressure. The platform's protections for the Congressional Budget Office and the Joint Committee on Taxation (Appendix C) matter more under this rule.
- **The parliamentarian decides what reconciliation can carry.** The tax conditions in Section 8.2.3 and parts of Chapter 5 may be ruled "merely incidental" and struck.
- **Repeal symmetry is only as strong as the next Senate.** A future majority could change the rules again by the same method used to adopt them.
- **Executive restraint gives up early wins.** Limiting executive action means visible results depend on Congress, which moves more slowly.
- **The sequence assumes unified government.** With a divided Congress, only the bipartisan track and limited executive actions are realistic.
