## Chapter 16: Criminal Justice & Public Safety

### 16.1 Guiding Principle

The first responsibility of the criminal justice system is public safety.

The second responsibility is rehabilitation whenever rehabilitation is realistically possible.

Punishment alone is not a public-safety strategy.

Reducing future crime is the ultimate objective.

### 16.2 Violent Crime Priority Standard \[Statute\]

Federal law enforcement shall prioritize:

- Violent offenders

- Sexual offenders

- Human traffickers

- Organized criminal enterprises

- Repeat predatory offenders

Resources should focus on individuals who pose the greatest danger to the public.

### 16.3 Rehabilitation First Framework \[Statute\]

Whenever public safety allows, non-violent offenders should have access to:

- Education

- Workforce development

- Mental-health treatment

- Substance-abuse treatment

- Reentry support

The purpose of rehabilitation is reducing future victimization.

### 16.4 Sentencing Review Commission \[Statute\]

The United States Sentencing Commission, an independent agency in the judicial branch, shall periodically review sentencing policies for:

- Fairness

- Consistency

- Cost effectiveness

- Public-safety outcomes

- Recidivism reduction

Recommendations shall be reported publicly.

### 16.5 Prison Reentry Initiative \[Statute\]

Individuals successfully completing sentences shall receive access to:

- Employment assistance

- Housing support

- Credential recognition

- Educational opportunities

- Workforce development

The objective is successful reintegration into society.

### 16.6 Ban on For-Profit Prisons & Detention \[Statute\]

The federal government shall terminate contracts with for-profit prison and immigration-detention operators. No federal correctional or immigration-detention facility shall be operated for private profit.

The deprivation of liberty should never become a profit-generating business model, and human incarceration should never create a business incentive to increase imprisonment.

### 16.7 Public Safety Outcome Standard \[Statute\]

Justice policies shall be evaluated based upon:

- Crime reduction

- Victim protection

- Public safety

- Reduced recidivism

rather than political ideology.

Cross-reference: See Section 14.6 Evidence-Based Governance Requirement.

### 16.8 Victim Rights Framework \[Statute\]

Victims of violent crime shall receive enhanced access to:

- Counseling services

- Legal support

- Restitution assistance

- Victim advocacy services

The justice system must protect both communities and victims while preserving constitutional rights for the accused.

### 16.9 Ending Qualified Immunity and Restoring Remedies \[Statute – High Resistance\]

The right to sue an officer who violates the Constitution should not depend on which government employs the officer. Federal law lets people sue state and local officers under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, but there is no federal statute for suits against federal officers, and the Supreme Court has declined to extend the court-created *Bivens* remedy to any new situation for 45 years \[1\]\[2\]. This section applies one set of rules to every officer: no qualified immunity, a capped personal share that the officer actually pays, and the government covering the rest.

#### 16.9.1 State and Local Officers

Qualified immunity, a judicially created doctrine, shall be eliminated by statute clarifying 42 U.S.C. § 1983. The employing government shall pay damages beyond the officer's personal share (Section 16.9.5).

#### 16.9.2 Federal Officers

Any person who, under color of federal law, deprives another person of rights secured by the Constitution of the United States shall be liable to the injured party in an action at law or suit in equity. The cause of action shall use the same liability, defenses, damages, attorney-fee, and enforcement rules as Section 1983 as revised by Section 16.9.1, so qualified immunity is not a defense. The officer's personal share is set by Section 16.9.5, and the United States shall pay the rest.

- **No categorical exceptions.** The cause of action covers immigration, border, and national security officers like any other federal officer. The existing state-secrets privilege continues to apply in individual cases.
- **Who pays.** Judgments and settlements are paid from the Judgment Fund (31 U.S.C. § 1304). The responsible agency shall repay the Fund from its own appropriated funds within two fiscal years, a period fixed in statute, as agencies already do for discrimination judgments, so that the cost falls on the agency whose officer caused the harm.

#### 16.9.3 Repairing the Federal Tort Claims Act

- **Discretionary function.** Conduct that violates the Constitution shall never be treated as a protected discretionary function. In *Martin v. United States* (2025), the Supreme Court left this question open for wrong-house raids and similar harms \[3\].
- **Judgment bar.** The rule that a judgment in a Tort Claims Act case ends all claims against the individual officer (28 U.S.C. § 2676) shall be repealed.
- **Exclusivity.** The Westfall Act shall not bar claims under Section 16.9.2 or 16.9.4.

#### 16.9.4 State Remedies Against Federal Officers

States may provide causes of action against federal officers for violations of the Constitution of the United States. These "converse-1983" laws have been enacted in several states and are being challenged in court \[4\]. The following limits apply:

- a state remedy may enforce only federal constitutional rights, and may not impose liability broader than the federal constitutional standard being enforced;
- the defenses, personal-share rules, and payment rules of this section apply;
- federal officers keep their right to move the case to federal court (28 U.S.C. § 1442).

These limits prevent a state from using its own law to target federal officers carrying out lawful federal duties, including civil rights and environmental enforcement.

#### 16.9.5 The Officer's Personal Share

- **Cap.** An officer's personal liability for a judgment or settlement shall not exceed $25,000, indexed to inflation.
- **No reimbursement.** The employing government may not pay or reimburse the officer's share, directly or indirectly, through indemnification, compensation adjustments, bonuses, or side agreements. Officers may buy personal liability insurance.
- **Bad faith.** If a jury finds that the officer violated the Constitution willfully or maliciously, the cap does not apply, punitive damages are available, and the employing government is not required to pay on the officer's behalf.

Colorado's 2020 law allowed cities to charge officers a share of judgments, but local governments did not use it \[5\]. Making the share mandatory is what gives it effect.

#### 16.9.6 Transition

Sections 16.9.1 to 16.9.5 apply to conduct occurring two years or more after enactment, so that local governments and their insurance pools can adjust coverage first.

#### 16.9.7 Public Reporting

Every settlement, judgment, and attorney-fee award in a case alleging a constitutional violation by a law enforcement officer shall be reported annually to a public database maintained by the Bureau of Justice Statistics, which has a mandatory duty to publish it. Each report shall list the agency, the amount, the category of allegation, and the outcome, with an anonymous identifier for each officer so that repeat cases can be tracked. No personally identifying information about claimants or officers shall be published.

Federal agencies report directly. State and local governments report as a condition of receiving federal justice grants. Any person may sue to compel the Bureau to perform its duty.

### 16.10 Firearm Safety \[Statute – High Resistance\]

Background checks shall be required for all firearm sales and transfers, including private and online sales, with narrow exceptions (such as transfers between close family members).

Extreme risk protection (red flag) orders shall allow a court to temporarily remove firearms from a person who poses a danger to themselves or others. Federal law and federal funding incentives shall support these orders, with due-process protections: notice, a hearing, a clear evidentiary standard, time limits, and a defined path to restore firearm rights.

Honest limit: these measures do not ban any category of firearm. They will face Second Amendment challenges, so due-process protections are essential, and enforcement depends on background-check system funding and compliance.

### 16.11 Federal Cannabis Legalization \[Statute\]

Cannabis shall be removed from the federal controlled-substances schedule.

States shall retain authority over regulation within their jurisdictions.

Federal authorities shall expunge qualifying non-violent federal cannabis records.

### 16.12 Civil Addiction Courts & Public Health Stabilization \[Statute\]

Low-level possession offenses shall be routed through civil diversion systems rather than traditional criminal incarceration.

Treatment participation must remain voluntary.

Completion of civil diversion shall result in expungement of the related record.

**Parity Lock**

Diversion systems require adequate treatment capacity before activation.

**Proximity Lock**

Treatment access must remain geographically accessible to affected populations.

Addiction Hotspots shall receive priority for treatment capacity so that diversion can activate there first.

Cross-reference: See Chapter 5 Universal Health Insurance.

### 16.13 Civil Rights Risk Reduction Grants \[Statute\]

Because Section 16.9 places liability costs on the governments that employ officers, those governments and their insurance pools shall have help reducing the misconduct that produces claims. Insurance pools have already pressed departments to change risky practices \[6\], and claims costs have risen where immunity was removed \[7\].

Grants shall support:

- body-worn cameras and evidence systems;
- early intervention systems that flag officers with repeated complaints;
- de-escalation and crisis-response training;
- independent investigation of deaths and serious injuries in custody;
- participation in a national database of decertified officers;
- modernization of municipal insurance pools, including risk-based premiums.

The grants shall be awarded within the existing federal policing grant programs (the Edward Byrne Memorial Justice Assistance Grant and Community Oriented Policing Services programs) and require no new appropriation. They operate within existing authorized appropriations and create no new phase-in obligation under Section 27.9. A federal backstop that pays judgments for local governments is rejected, because it would weaken the incentive to prevent misconduct.

### 16.14 Pattern-or-Practice Enforcement \[Statute\]

Enforcement against a pattern or practice of unconstitutional policing shall not depend on which administration controls the Department of Justice.

- **State attorneys general** shall have concurrent authority to bring suits under 34 U.S.C. § 12601.
- **Published reasons.** When the Department of Justice abandons a pattern-or-practice case or moves to end a consent decree before its terms are met, it shall publish written reasons and notify Congress, as Section 13.7 requires for other civil rights cases.

### 16.15 Funding \[Statute\]

| Measure | Paid by |
| --- | --- |
| Federal officer judgments and settlements (16.9.2) | Judgment Fund, repaid by the responsible agency from its own appropriations |
| State and local judgments and settlements (16.9.1) | The employing government and its insurance pool |
| Public reporting (16.9.7) | Bureau of Justice Statistics base appropriations |
| Civil Rights Risk Reduction Grants (16.13) | Within existing federal policing grant programs; no new appropriation |

Judgments are liabilities, not programs, and do not draw on any revenue stream assigned in Section 27.4.

### 16.16 Honest Limits

- **Insurance costs will rise.** New Mexico's county insurance pool reported about $30 million in settlements and legal costs in the first years after the state removed qualified immunity \[7\]. The two-year transition and the risk reduction grants soften this but will not prevent it.
- **Settlement costs remain difficult to forecast.** The long-run effects depend on future litigation patterns, court interpretations, insurance markets, local policies, and agency behavior, and have not been formally scored.
- **Federal officer liability will be fought as an immigration issue.** Law enforcement unions and the Department of Homeland Security will oppose it, and it will be described as an attack on immigration enforcement.
- **State remedies are legally unsettled.** Courts have not decided whether states may create causes of action against federal officers, and the Justice Department is challenging Illinois's law \[4\]. Express congressional authorization in Section 16.9.4 is designed to settle that question.
- **The personal share is modest.** A $25,000 cap may not deter every officer, and personal insurance will soften it further. The bad-faith rule is the stronger deterrent.
