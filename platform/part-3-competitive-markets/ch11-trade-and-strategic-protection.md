## Chapter 11: Trade & Strategic Protection

### 11.1 Guiding Principle

Trade is a tool for prosperity, not an ideology.

The United States should pursue:

- Free trade where beneficial

- Fair trade where necessary

- Strategic protections where justified

Trade policy should strengthen both economic growth and national resilience.

### 11.2 Fair Trade & Economic Partnership Act \[Statute\]

The United States shall pursue mutually beneficial trade relationships with allies and trusted partners.

#### 11.2.1 Allied Trade Framework

Trade agreements should encourage:

- Democratic governance

- Labor standards

- Environmental standards

- Intellectual-property protections

- Fair competition

#### 11.2.2 Strategic Tariff Authority

Targeted tariffs may be imposed against foreign governments engaging in:

- Intellectual property theft

- Currency manipulation

- Forced technology transfer

- State-supported dumping

- Trade discrimination

Tariffs under this section shall be imposed only under authority expressly granted by Congress, subject to congressional review, and shall meet the Strategic Protection Test (see Definitions). The Supreme Court held in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* (2026) that emergency-powers law does not authorize tariffs; this section makes the authority explicit and accountable.

#### 11.2.3 Supply Chain Protection Standard

Trade restrictions shall be designed to minimize unnecessary disruptions to American businesses dependent upon global supply chains.

Policies must carefully balance:

- National security

- Consumer costs

- Manufacturing competitiveness

#### 11.2.4 Strategic Industry Protection

Special consideration may be given to:

- Semiconductors

- Pharmaceuticals

- Critical minerals

- Energy infrastructure

- Defense technologies

Priority sectors are defined in Section 21.5; this section governs trade treatment only. Any protection shall meet the Strategic Protection Test.

Cross-reference: See Chapter 21 Infrastructure, Industry & Supply Chains.

### 11.3 Honest Limits

- **Americans pay most of the cost of tariffs.** Studies of the 2018 tariffs found complete pass-through of the tariffs into U.S. import prices, so the cost fell on American consumers and businesses rather than foreign exporters \[1\]. Tariffs under Section 11.2.2 will raise prices for buyers of the targeted goods, and trading partners may retaliate against U.S. exports, including farm exports (Chapter 10).
- **Accountability makes tariffs slower.** In *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* (2026), the Supreme Court held that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize the President to impose tariffs \[2\]. Requiring express authority from Congress for each tariff makes protection accountable but slow, and Congress may not act at all.
- **The Strategic Protection Test depends on contested evidence.** Whether a foreign practice amounts to dumping, currency manipulation, or forced technology transfer is often disputed, and domestic industries will press to qualify. The test's cost cap and sunset limit the damage of a wrong call; they do not prevent it.
- **Labor and environmental standards are hard to enforce abroad.** Standards in trade agreements work only through dispute and enforcement mechanisms that partners accept, and enforcement of such standards has historically been uneven.
- **Tariff revenue is not platform financing.** Strategic tariff receipts go to the General Fund and are never counted toward any program (Section 27.3), because a tariff that changes a partner's behavior shrinks its own revenue.
- **Partners may challenge these tariffs.** Tariffs justified on security or unfair-trade grounds can be contested under international trade agreements.
